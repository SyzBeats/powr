# Progression Models, Periodization, Autoregulation (incl. VBT), Deloads, Tapering, Detraining and Realistic Rates of Progress

Scope note: These notes are for a training planner/tracker PWA for bodybuilders and powerlifters. Out of scope here (other researchers cover them): 1RM prediction formulas, RPE/RIR scale definitions, weekly set-volume dose-response, rest/tempo/exercise selection, nutrition. Abstract-level data was pulled via PubMed/Europe PMC. Full texts were read for: Bell 2023, Rogerson 2024, Coleman 2024, Latella 2020, Renner 2024 and parts of Jukic 2023. "Inference" bullets are my synthesis for app design. They are not findings.

---

## 1. Periodization: periodized vs non-periodized; linear vs undulating (DUP/WUP) vs block — does it matter for trained lifters?

### Takeaway
When volume is equated, periodized programs give a small extra gain in 1RM strength over non-periodized ones (ES ≈ 0.3–0.4). They give no extra hypertrophy. Undulating may beat linear for strength in trained lifters, but the confidence interval is wide (lower bound ≈ 0). Linear and DUP produce the same hypertrophy. Block periodization has only small trials. For an app, the periodization model matters less than managing volume, effort and specificity. A structured plan plus a peaking phase for powerlifters is enough. Hypertrophy users get no measurable benefit from model choice.

### Cited Findings
- **Moesgaard et al. 2022** (Sports Med 52(7):1647–1666; DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01636-1; PMID 35044672). Meta-analysis of 35 volume-equated studies.
  - Periodized vs non-periodized, 1RM: ES 0.31 (95% CI 0.04–0.57), p = 0.02, favoring periodized.
  - Same comparison, hypertrophy: ES 0.13 (−0.10 to 0.36), p = 0.27 (no difference).
  - Undulating vs linear, 1RM: ES 0.31 (0.02–0.61), p = 0.04, favoring undulating. Trained subgroup ES 0.61 (0.00–1.22), p = 0.05. Untrained subgroup ES 0.06 (−0.20 to 0.31), no difference.
  - Undulating vs linear, hypertrophy: ES 0.05 (−0.20 to 0.29), no difference.
  - The authors suggest the strength benefit comes from neural adaptation, not muscle growth.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); [Europe PMC abstract data](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:35044672&resultType=core&format=json); [Univ. Copenhagen press release](https://nexs.ku.dk/english/news/2022/varied-strength-training-programmes-increase-strength-but-not-muscle-mass/)
- **Williams et al. 2017** (Sports Med 47(10):2083–2100; DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0734-y; PMID 28497285). Meta-analysis of 18 studies (1988–2015), 81 effects.
  - Periodized vs non-periodized, 1RM: ES 0.43 (95% CI 0.27–0.58), p < 0.001.
  - Significant moderators: periodization model (β = 0.51), training status (β = −0.59, i.e., larger benefit in **untrained**), study length (β = 0.03 per week) and training frequency (β = 0.46).
  - A published comment (Nunes et al. 2018, PMID 29178086) disputed aspects of the analysis.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/); [Nunes comment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29178086/)
- **Rhea & Alderman 2004** (Res Q Exerc Sport 75(4):413–422; PMID 15673040). The older meta-analysis.
  - Overall ES 0.84 (±1.41) favoring periodized training.
  - After controlling for volume, frequency and intensity, the effect of variation alone shrank to ES 0.25.
  - Moderators: age, training status, program length.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15673040/)
- **Harries et al. 2015** (J Strength Cond Res 29(4):1113–1125; PMID 25268290). 17 studies, 510 participants.
  - No difference between linear and undulating for upper- or lower-body strength.
  - Only 5 of the studies found a significant between-group difference.
  - The authors flag short study durations and few athletic samples.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/)
- **Grgic et al. 2017** (PeerJ 5:e3695; DOI 10.7717/peerj.3695; PMID 28848690). 13 studies: linear vs DUP on hypertrophy.
  - Pooled d = −0.02 (95% CI −0.25 to 0.21), p = 0.85.
  - The authors call for more trials in trained people that use ultrasound or MRI.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28848690/)
- **Evans 2019** (Front Physiol 10:13; PMID 30728780). Narrative mini-review.
  - Periodized training appears superior for strength regardless of training status.
  - Undulating may give a greater strength benefit.
  - Linear ≈ undulating for hypertrophy (mainly untrained data). Any hypertrophy benefit of periodization is unclear.
  - Note: this is not a powerlifting-specific review.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728780/)
- **Block periodization trials (small, trained non-powerlifters):**
  - Bartolomei et al. 2014 (JSCR): 24 resistance-trained men, block vs traditional with volume equated. Block gave greater upper-body power. No between-group difference in lower-body strength. [CRIS record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/a-comparison-of-traditional-and-block-periodized-strength-trainin/)
  - Bartolomei et al. 2016 (Kinesiology): 18 trained strength/power athletes, block vs weekly undulating. Block gave greater bench press gains (p = 0.040). No difference in lower-body isometric strength or lean mass. [Hrčak](https://hrcak.srce.hr/160744)
- **Rhea et al. 2002** (JSCR; PMID 11991778): 20 men randomized to linear or DUP. Bench and leg press 1RM gains significantly favored DUP. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11991778/)
- **Practice data, powerlifters' peaking structure:** in a survey of 364 North-American raw powerlifters, peak volume most often fell 5–8 weeks before a meet and peak intensity about 2 weeks before. Experienced lifters therefore commonly use a block or phase structure that moves from volume to intensity. [Travis et al. 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34846328/)

### Inferences
- The periodization effects are small (ES 0.3–0.6 for strength, ≈0 for hypertrophy) and their CIs are wide. Do not hard-code one "best" model. Offer linear, DUP/WUP and block templates as user-selectable options. The engine's value lies in progression, autoregulation and fatigue management.
- **Default templates.**
  - **Hypertrophy users:** use any model. Progress via reps/load (double progression) and manage effort and volume. Expect no difference between models.
  - **Powerlifters:** phase/block structure (accumulation → intensification → peak/taper). Within each week, undulate across sessions (e.g., heavy/moderate/light days). This mirrors the surveyed practice of peak volume 5–8 weeks out and peak intensity about 2 weeks out.
- The strongest signal is specific to trained lifters and strength (UP > LP, ES 0.61, CI lower bound 0.00). It is a weak reason to default intermediate and advanced strength users to undulating loading within the week rather than pure week-to-week linear loading.
- Mesocycle length: no meta-analysis tests an optimal block length. The common 3–6 week accumulation blocks followed by a deload (see Section 4) are practice-based.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis directly compares block periodization against DUP or linear in competitive powerlifters. Evidence is limited to small trials in other strength/power athletes (Bartolomei 2014, 2016).
- I did not retrieve a powerlifting-specific periodization review by "Evans 2019". The Evans 2019 paper found is a general mini-review in Front Physiol. If the coordinator meant another paper, it needs a separate check.
- Afonso et al. 2019 ("Is empirical research on periodization trustworthy?") and similar methodological critiques were not retrieved. Their arguments (short studies, untrained samples, poor equating) are relevant caveats but were not verified in this session.
- The evidence is mostly from studies of ≤12–16 weeks. Periodization effects over months to years in trained lifters are essentially untested.

## 2. Progression rules: double progression, fixed % increments, APRE, RPE/RIR-based autoregulation; load vs rep progression; typical increments

### Takeaway
Autoregulated load prescription (RPE/RIR, APRE, velocity) is at least as effective as fixed %1RM loading. Some meta-analyses find a small to moderate strength advantage for autoregulation (Zhang 2021 ES 0.64 in athletes), but Hickmott 2022 found autoregulated and standardized loading similar. Progressing reps at a constant load gives the same hypertrophy as progressing load (Plotkin 2022), with a trivial strength edge for load progression. The only formal increment guidance is ACSM's "2–10% when 1–2 reps above target". Absolute increments must shrink with training age, because advanced lifters gain only about 1–2 kg per month on a given lift.

### Cited Findings
- **ACSM Position Stand 2009, "Progression models in resistance training for healthy adults"** (Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687–708; PMID 19204579).
  - Increase load 2–10% when the lifter can complete the current workload for 1–2 reps over the target.
  - Novice: 8–12RM. Intermediate (about 6 months of consistent training): 1–12RM, periodized, with heavy loads (1–6RM) later on.
  - Frequency: 2–3 days/wk (novice), 3–4 (intermediate), 4–5 (advanced).
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
  - The 2002 version (PMID 11828249) used the same 2–10% rule. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11828249/)
- **Plotkin et al. 2022** (PeerJ 10:e14142; DOI 10.7717/peerj.14142; PMID 36199287). RCT, 43 trained participants (≥1 yr), 8 weeks, 4 sets × 4 lower-body exercises, 2×/wk.
  - LOAD group (n = 22) increased load at constant reps. REPS group (n = 21) increased reps at constant load.
  - Rectus femoris growth slightly favored REPS: +2.8 mm (90% CI −0.5 to 5.8).
  - Smith-squat 1RM slightly favored LOAD: +2.0 kg (90% CI −2.4 to 7.8), of "questionable practical significance".
  - All other differences were trivial.
  - Conclusion: both progression styles are viable.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)
- **Helms et al. 2018** (Front Physiol 9:247; DOI 10.3389/fphys.2018.00247; PMID 29628895). 21 trained men, 8 weeks, squat and bench 3×/wk, DUP, sets and reps matched.
  - %1RM group (n = 11) vs RPE group (n = 10).
  - Squat 1RM: +13.9 ± 5.9 kg vs +17.1 ± 5.4 kg. Bench: +9.6 ± 5.4 kg vs +10.7 ± 3.3 kg. Both comparisons non-significant.
  - Magnitude-based inference: squat ES 0.50 (90% CL ±0.63) favoring RPE.
  - Muscle thickness was similar between groups.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/)
- **Graham & Cleather 2021** (JSCR 35(9):2451–2456; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003164; PMID 31009432). 31 resistance-trained males, 12 weeks, squat program 2×/wk, volume matched.
  - RIR-autoregulated vs fixed loading with theoretically equal intensity.
  - Front squat: +11.7% vs +8.3% (p = 0.004). Back squat: +10.8% vs +7.1%.
  - The RIR group ended up training at a higher intensity relative to post-test 1RM by week 12.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31009432/); [St Mary's repository](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067)
- **Zhang et al. 2021** (Front Physiol 12:651112; DOI 10.3389/fphys.2021.651112; PMID 33776802). Meta-analysis of 8 studies, 166 athletes with ≥1 yr training, 5–10 week interventions.
  - Autoregulation beat fixed loading for maximal strength: ES 0.64 (p < 0.001, I² = 0%).
  - ≤8-week interventions: ES 0.87. 8–10 weeks: ES 0.32.
  - APRE ES 0.78, the most effective of the autoregulation types.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33776802/)
- **Larsen et al. 2021** (PeerJ 9:e10663; DOI 10.7717/peerj.10663; PMID 33520457). Systematic review of 14 studies, 30 groups, 356 participants.
  - All autoregulation protocols increased 1RM (small to large effects). These included subjective methods (RIR, RPE, flexible DUP) and objective ones (velocity targets, velocity loss).
  - No pooled effect.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33520457/)
- **Hickmott et al. 2022** (Sports Med Open 8:9; DOI 10.1186/s40798-021-00404-9; PMID 35038063). Meta-analysis of 15 studies (6 load-autoregulation, 9 volume-autoregulation).
  - **Autoregulated and standardized load prescription gave similar 1RM gains.**
  - Velocity-loss (VL) thresholds ≤25% beat >25% for strength. This was significant only when sets and relative intensity were equated.
  - VL >25% beat ≤25% for hypertrophy, but did not beat 20–25%.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)
- **Mann et al. 2010, APRE** (JSCR 24(7):1718–1723; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181def4a6; PMID 20543732). 23 NCAA Division I football players (2.65 ± 0.8 training years), 6 weeks pre-season.
  - APRE (n = 12) vs linear periodization (n = 11).
  - Bench 1RM change: +93.4 ± 103 vs −0.4 ± 49.6 (p = 0.02). The abstract gives units as N, which appears to be a units error.
  - Estimated squat 1RM change: +192.7 ± 199 vs +37.2 ± 155 (p = 0.05).
  - Reps at 225 lb: +3.17 vs −0.09 (p = 0.02).
  - Caveats: very short, small, and in-season athletes.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20543732/)
- **APRE 6RM protocol** (as tabulated in a 2023 PeerJ trial):
  - Set 1: 10 reps at 50% of 6RM. Set 2: 6 reps at 75% of 6RM. Set 3: as many reps as possible (AMRAP) at the 6RM load.
  - Set 4 load and the next session's 6RM are adjusted from set-3 reps:
    - 0–2 reps → −2.5 to −5 kg
    - 3–4 reps → 0 to −2.5 kg
    - 5–7 reps → no change
    - 8–12 reps → +2.5 to +5 kg
    - ≥13 reps → +5 to +10 kg
  - A secondary source gives +5 to +7.5 kg for ≥13 reps. 3RM and 10RM variants exist.
  - [Huang et al. 2023 PeerJ table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10414022/table/table-2); [Training & Conditioning overview](https://training-conditioning.com/article/understanding-apre-part-1/)
- **Realistic progression in elite athletes** (useful scale for "advanced" increments). McMaster et al. 2013 (Sports Med 43(5):367–384; PMID 23529287) reviewed 27 studies, 1,015 elite rugby and American-football players.
  - Strength rose about 0.42% per session at moderate intensity-relative volume.
  - Weekly frequency per muscle group gave about 0.9% (2×/wk), 1.8% (3×/wk) and 1.3% (4×/wk) strength gain per week.
  - Long-term gains: +7.1% at 12 months, +8.5% at 24 months, +12.5% at 48 months.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)

### Inferences
- **Recommended progression engine (layered):**
  1. **Double progression (default for accessories and hypertrophy work).** Prescribe a rep range (e.g., 8–12) at a target RIR. When all sets reach the top of the range at or above the target RIR, increase load by the smallest practical increment and reset to the bottom of the range. Plotkin 2022 supports rep progression as equally effective for hypertrophy. ACSM's "1–2 reps above target → +2–10%" is the formal anchor.
  2. **RPE/RIR-based load selection (default for main lifts, intermediate and above).** Load = e1RM × %-for-(reps, target RPE). Update e1RM from each top set. Helms 2018, Graham & Cleather 2021 and Zhang 2021 support this as non-inferior or slightly superior to fixed %.
  3. **APRE (optional preset).** Implement the 3/6/10RM tables above verbatim.
  4. **Fixed % progression (beginners, or users who want it).** Linear weekly load increases with an automatic stall rule, e.g., two failed sessions → reduce 5–10% and rebuild. The 5–10% reset is a practitioner convention, not tested.
- **Increment sizing (inference).** Scale increments to the expected rate of gain (Section 7) and to the minimum plate increments available.
  - **Novice:** about +2.5–5 kg per session on lower-body compounds and +1–2.5 kg on upper-body (≈2–5%).
  - **Intermediate:** weekly increases of about 1–2.5%.
  - **Advanced:** e1RM rises only about 0.5–1% per month, so progress mostly through reps, sets and RPE at a given load, with load jumps every 2–4 weeks.
  - Support microplates (0.25–0.5 kg per side) and user-defined plate inventories.
- An advanced powerlifter whose total grows about 1 kg/week (Latella 2020, Section 7) cannot sustain +2.5 kg/week on every lift. The app should detect when prescribed increases exceed the user's observed e1RM trend and switch to rep/RPE-based progression.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares double progression against fixed % or RPE progression directly. No study tests specific increment sizes (e.g., 2.5 vs 5 kg) by training level. Increment recommendations beyond ACSM's 2–10% are practitioner-derived.
- I could not retrieve the Graham & Cleather 2021 back-squat p-value or the exact RIR prescription.
- The APRE table values differ slightly between secondary sources. The original Siff/Mann tables were not retrieved.

## 3. Velocity-based training (VBT): load–velocity profiles, MVTs, velocity-loss thresholds, RIR/1RM estimation from velocity, reliability, smartphone camera apps

### Takeaway
Mean concentric velocity is tightly linked to %1RM (R² ≈ 0.96–0.98) and fairly stable within a person, so it can autoregulate daily loads. Minimum velocity thresholds (MVT) differ by exercise and by population:
- squat ≈ 0.23–0.32 m/s
- bench ≈ 0.10–0.17 m/s
- deadlift ≈ 0.14 m/s

The individual velocity at 1RM is unreliable (CV ≈ 22%), so 1RM prediction from load–velocity profiles overestimates and varies widely. On velocity loss:
- 1RM strength is similar across VL thresholds; about 20–30% may be optimal.
- Higher VL (>20–25%) gives more hypertrophy (per 1% VL, b = +0.006).
- Lower VL (≤15–20%) preserves jump and speed and is more time-efficient.

The best smartphone app (Qwik VBT) matched a linear transducer on iPhone under lab conditions. Other apps (Metric, MyLift) had larger errors and missed reps. No Android (e.g., Galaxy S22) camera-VBT validation was found.

### Cited Findings
**Load–velocity relationship and MVTs**
- **González-Badillo & Sánchez-Medina 2010** (Int J Sports Med 31(5):347–352; DOI 10.1055/s-0030-1248333; PMID 20180176). Bench press, 120 strength-trained males.
  - Mean propulsive velocity (MPV) vs %1RM: R² = 0.98.
  - Mean velocity at 1RM: 0.16 ± 0.04 m/s.
  - After 6 weeks of training, 1RM rose 9.3% but the velocity at each %1RM stayed stable. Velocity can therefore stand in for %1RM even as 1RM changes.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20180176/)
- **Sánchez-Medina et al. 2017** (Sports Med Int Open 1(2):E80–E88; DOI 10.1055/s-0043-102933; PMID 30539090). Full squat (Smith machine), 80 strength-trained men.
  - MV and MPV vs %1RM: R² = 0.96. Peak velocity: R² = 0.79, SEE 0.14 vs 0.06 m/s.
  - MV at 1RM: 0.32 ± 0.03 m/s.
  - The MV-based equation was restated in a published response letter: %1RM = 7.5786·MV² − 75.865·MV + 113.02. Verify against the original before coding.
  - [Thieme PDF](https://www.thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/s-0043-102933.pdf); [response letter, PMC6260059](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6260059)
- **Helms et al. 2017** (JSCR 31(2):292–297; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001517; PMID 27243918). 15 powerlifters (12 M / 3 F), free-weight competition lifts.
  - Average concentric velocity at 1RM: **squat 0.23 ± 0.05, bench 0.10 ± 0.04, deadlift 0.14 ± 0.05 m/s**.
  - Velocity vs RPE: r = −0.79 to −0.87. Velocity vs %1RM: r = −0.90 to −0.92.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27243918/)
- **Banyard et al. 2017** (JSCR 31(7):1897–1904; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001657; PMID 27669192). 17 strength-trained males, three 1RM back-squat sessions.
  - Actual 1RM was reliable: CV 2.1%, SEM 2.9 kg.
  - **Velocity at 1RM was 0.24 ± 0.06 m/s but unreliable**: ICC 0.42, CV 22.5%.
  - The best load–velocity 1RM prediction (5 loads up to 90%) still differed significantly from actual 1RM (ES 0.71). Individual errors ranged −5.5% to +27.8%, vs −5.6% to +4.8% for retesting the actual 1RM.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27669192/)

**Velocity → RIR (proximity to failure)**
- **Morán-Navarro et al. 2019** (JSCR 33(6):1496–1504; PMID 29944141). 30 men (novice, well-trained, highly trained) took sets to failure at 65/75/85% 1RM in bench, squat, prone row and shoulder press.
  - Velocities at 2, 4, 6 and 8 RIR were similar across loads (CV 4.4–8.0%).
  - Training level made no difference.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29944141/)
- **Jukic et al. 2024** (Physiol Rep; DOI 10.14814/phy2.15955; PMC10901726). 46 trained adults, back squat.
  - **Individual** RIR–velocity models fitted better than general models.
  - Individual models predicted RIR in a later session with mean error <2 reps. General models did not reach that accuracy.
  - A single model pooled across loads performed similarly to load-specific ones.
  - [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10901726)

**Velocity loss (VL) thresholds**
- **Sánchez-Medina & González-Badillo 2011** (MSSE 43(9):1725–1734; PMID 21311352). 18 trained males.
  - Within-set VL correlated with lactate (r = 0.93–0.97) and with CMJ height loss and velocity loss at the 1 m/s load (r = 0.91–0.97).
  - Bench showed greater VL than squat for comparable effort.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21311352/)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 27(7):724–735; PMID 27038416). 22 young males, 8 weeks of squat, VL20 vs VL40.
  - Strength gains were similar.
  - CMJ improved +9.5% (VL20) vs +3.5% (VL40), with VL20 doing **40% fewer reps**.
  - VL40 gave greater vastus lateralis/intermedius hypertrophy but reduced type IIX myosin heavy chain. VL20 preserved IIX.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27038416/)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2020** (MSSE 52(8):1752–1762; PMID 32049887). 64 trained men randomized to VL0, VL10, VL20 or VL40; full squat at 70–85% 1RM, 3 sets, 2×/wk for 8 weeks.
  - Strength, sprint and jump gains were similar across groups despite different volumes.
  - Hypertrophy was significant only in VL20 and VL40.
  - VL40 impaired early rate of force development.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32049887/)
- **Jukic et al. 2023** (Sports Med 53(1):177–214; DOI 10.1007/s40279-022-01754-4; PMC9807551). Systematic review and meta-analysis: 18 acute and 19 longitudinal studies (median 8 weeks), 735 participants (656 M / 69 F), mostly back squat on a Smith machine.
  - VL did **not** influence strength or endurance gains.
  - Per 1% increase in VL: hypertrophy b = +0.006 (95% CI 0.001–0.012), CMJ b = −0.040, velocity at submaximal loads b = −0.018, and sprint performance worsened.
  - In the squat, **20% VL ≈ 50% of possible reps**. 40–50% VL ≈ reps to or near failure.
  - VL is not equivalent across exercises or loads. It also depends on the reference rep (first vs fastest) and the velocity metric (mean vs peak).
  - [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9807551); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s40279-022-01754-4)
- **Zhang et al. 2023** (Healthcare 11(3):337; DOI 10.3390/healthcare11030337; PMID 36766912). Dose-response meta-analysis of 9 studies, 336 trained males (≥1 yr).
  - The VL vs 1RM-gain curve is an inverted U. **20–30% VL may be optimal for maximal strength.**
  - Strength gained per rep declines linearly as VL rises.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36766912/)
- Hickmott 2022 (Section 2): VL ≤25% better for strength when sets and intensity are equated. VL >25% better for hypertrophy, though not better than 20–25%. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35038063/)

**Smartphone / camera-based VBT validity**
- **Renner, Mitter & Baca 2024** (PLOS ONE 19(11):e0313919; DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0313919; PMC11575817). 20 competitive powerlifters (14 M / 6 F); squat, bench and deadlift singles at 45–90%; 589 reps; criterion Vicon 3D motion capture at 200 Hz.
  - All apps ran on **iPhone SE 2022 (iOS only)**.
  - **Qwik VBT:** systematic bias ≈ 0–0.21 (standardized), RMSE 0.01–0.04 m/s, **no missed reps**. Equivalent to the RepOne linear transducer in all lifts except the deadlift slope.
  - **Metric VBT:** RMSE up to 0.14 m/s (acceptable only in bench, RMSE 0.04). Missed 52 reps and recorded 16 ghost reps.
  - **MyLift (My Jump Lab):** missed 175 reps, including 168 of the bench reps (only about 16% recorded).
  - Authors' caveats: results apply to the tested app versions and to ideal conditions (tripod, standardized camera position, good lighting). Qwik requires manual plate selection.
  - [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11575817)
- **Metric VBT vs Vicon, PeerJ 2024:**
  - Detected >95% of reps.
  - Poor-to-moderate agreement for mean velocity.
  - iOS/iPadOS only.
  - [PeerJ 17789](https://peerj.com/articles/17789)
- **Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2017** (Sensors): the PowerLift app (iOS) and the Beast wearable were reported valid and reliable for several lifts. [PMC5581394](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5581394/)
- A search summary of a systematic review of six commercial smartphone VBT apps reported performance varying by app, exercise and load, and lower validity than dedicated devices. I did not read it in full; the source surfaced via search was [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/bitstreams/c29c52ac-01ca-46cf-a7b5-df6e6661f9f6/download).

### Inferences
- **Default MVT table (free-weight, mean concentric velocity).** These are population priors. Learn individual values from logged near-max sets.
  - **Squat 0.23–0.30 m/s.** Powerlifters ≈0.23. Smith-machine full squat in non-powerlifters ≈0.32.
  - **Bench 0.10–0.17 m/s.**
  - **Deadlift ≈0.14 m/s.**
- **Do NOT use the load–velocity profile for 1RM testing or prediction** (Banyard 2017: up to +28% error). Use it for **daily load adjustment** at submaximal loads (e.g., 60–85% 1RM), where velocity–%1RM is linear and stable.
  - **Readiness check (inference):** compare the warm-up velocity at a fixed load (e.g., about 70–80% e1RM) with the user's rolling baseline. If velocity is down by more than about 0.05–0.06 m/s, or more than 1 SD of the user's day-to-day variation, scale today's top-set load down by about 2.5–5%. If it is up by a similar margin, allow +2.5%. Thresholds must exceed the device error, so camera apps need wider bands.
- **VL-based set termination presets (inference from Jukic 2023, Pareja-Blanco 2017/2020, Hickmott 2022, Zhang 2023):**
  - **Powerlifting strength/peaking:** VL 10–20% on main lifts. Strength is equal, fatigue is lower, and it preserves rate of force development and IIX fibers.
  - **General strength:** 20–30% (Zhang 2023 optimum).
  - **Hypertrophy:** 25–40%. Alternatively use more sets at 20–25% VL, which matched >25% in Hickmott 2022.
  - The app must store VL per exercise. Bench reaches a given VL faster than squat for the same effort.
- **RIR from velocity:** fit a per-user, per-exercise RIR–velocity model from logged sets to failure or with RIR ratings. Morán-Navarro 2019 and Jukic 2024 show velocity at a given RIR is load-independent. Until about 2–3 calibration sets exist, use population defaults and display them as low-confidence.
- **Galaxy S22 / camera VBT:** because no Android validation exists, treat in-app camera velocity as approximate (error likely ≥0.03–0.05 m/s).
  - Require a side-on tripod view, plate-diameter calibration and good lighting.
  - Prefer VL within a set, which is a relative measure and more robust to scaling bias than absolute MVT.
  - Offer a Bluetooth linear-transducer or IMU import path as the "high-accuracy" option.
  - Close to 1RM, an error of 0.03 m/s against a bench MVT of 0.10 m/s is about 30% relative error, so do not stop sets automatically on absolute MVT for bench and deadlift.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed validation of any Android camera-based VBT app** was found, and none on the Samsung Galaxy S22. App-store availability (e.g., whether Qwik VBT or My Jump Lab run on Android) was not verified.
- The Weakley et al. 2021 review ("Velocity-Based Training: From Theory to Application", Strength Cond J) was not retrieved. Its MVT tables and VL recommendations are not included here.
- The pooled effect sizes in Jukic 2023 for the low/moderate/high VL categories sit in the part of the full text I did not read. Only the meta-regression slopes are reported above.
- Most VL studies use the Smith-machine squat and bench with young trained men. Evidence for the deadlift, women and highly advanced powerlifters is sparse.
- The exact coefficients of the Sánchez-Medina 2017 equation come from a response letter and should be verified against the original table.

## 4. Deloads: definition, frequency, magnitude (volume/intensity/effort), duration, planned vs reactive

### Takeaway
Deloading is near-universal among strength and physique athletes, but the evidence base is mainly expert consensus and surveys.
- **Typical practice:** about every 5–6 weeks (5.6 ± 2.3), lasting about 1 week (6.4 ± 1.7 days).
- **Main changes:** volume falls by cutting sets (and reps), effort falls (more RIR), load usually drops somewhat, and frequency and exercise selection stay the same.
- **Scheduling:** both planned and reactive/autoregulated deloads are endorsed.

The only RCT (Coleman 2024) tested complete cessation as the deload. It found no hypertrophy benefit and a small strength cost, which argues against full-rest deloads when strength is the goal.

### Cited Findings
- **Bell et al. 2023, Delphi consensus** (Sports Med Open 9:87; DOI 10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0; PMID 37730925).
  - Panel: 34 → 29 → 21 coaches over 3 rounds (>3 yrs coaching; about 81% powerlifting). Consensus threshold ≥70%.
  - **Proposed definition** (written by the authors, not voted on): "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training".
  - **Frequency items:**
    - "Deloading could be included in each mesocycle": 100%.
    - "Frequency may depend on how the athlete responds": 100%.
  - **No consensus on a specific duration.**
  - **Volume:**
    - Lower sets: 100%.
    - Lower reps: 100%.
    - Cut training days: 100%.
    - Fewer exercises: 95%.
    - Use a minimum effective dose: 90.5%.
  - **Effort:** "reduction in proximity to failure": 100%.
  - **Intensity:**
    - Intensity "could remain the same, but volume may drop": 95.2%.
    - Intensity could drop over consecutive days: 100%.
    - In non-competitive periods, both volume and intensity could decrease: 100%.
  - **Exercise selection:**
    - Selection could remain unchanged: 100%.
    - Keep multi-joint lifts: 100%.
    - Remove assistance exercises: 85.7%.
  - **Planning:** planned, autoregulatory and both were each endorsed at 100%.
  - **Triggers:** deload when fatigued regardless of mesocycle week: 100%, but this item was borderline-unstable.
  - Deloads should **not** differ by sex: 84.6% disagreed that they should.
  - **Limitations:** expert opinion only; 38% attrition; athletes not consulted.
  - [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10511399/fullTextXML); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)
- **Rogerson et al. 2024, cross-sectional survey** (Sports Med Open 10:26; DOI 10.1186/s40798-024-00691-y; PMID 38499934).
  - Sample: 246 competitive athletes (73.6% male; 63.4% powerlifting, 18.3% physique), 8.2 ± 6.2 yrs training.
  - **Frequency: every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks** (range 1–12). **Duration: 6.4 ± 1.7 days** (range 1–14).
  - By sport (frequency / duration):
    - Powerlifting: 5.5 ± 2.2 wk / 6.5 ± 1.6 d.
    - Physique: about 5.8 wk / 6.3 ± 2.1 d.
    - Weightlifting: 4.8 wk / 8.5 d.
  - **Timing:**
    - Pre-planned only: 47.2%.
    - Planned plus reactive: 39.4%.
    - Reactive only: 13.4%.
  - **Triggers:**
    - Program specifies it: 65.4%.
    - Feeling "beat up": 62.6%.
    - Performance stalls or declines: 54.1%.
  - **Changes during a deload:**
    - Weekly sets: decreased in 78.9%.
    - Reps per set: decreased in 52.8%.
    - Session frequency: unchanged in 63.0%.
    - Multi-joint load: decreased in 83.7%.
    - Multi-joint effort (RPE/RIR): decreased in 84.9%.
    - Exercise selection: unchanged in about 64–70%.
    - Range of motion: unchanged in about 90%.
  - 65% felt they could progress without deloading.
  - No percentage magnitudes of reduction were reported.
  - [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10948666/fullTextXML); [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/38499934/)
- **Bell et al. 2022, coach interviews** (Front Sports Act Living): qualitative interviews with 18 strength and physique coaches about their deloading experiences. [PMC9811819](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9811819/)
- **Coleman et al. 2024, RCT** (PeerJ 12:e16777; DOI 10.7717/peerj.16777; PMID 38274324).
  - Sample: 39 trained young adults (29 M / 10 F; ≥1 yr training, 3.2–3.7 yrs mean).
  - 9-week upper/lower program, 4 sessions/wk, 5 sets of 8–12RM **to failure**, about 90 sets/wk.
  - The DELOAD group **fully stopped training in week 5**.
  - Results (DELOAD vs TRAD):
    - Smith-squat 1RM: −3.6 kg (95% CrI −10.4 to 3.2), P(favoring TRAD) = 0.85.
    - Isometric knee extension: −14.4 N·m (CrI −34.3 to 5.8), P = 0.92.
    - Muscle thickness, local endurance and CMJ: no meaningful differences.
    - The DELOAD group reported more soreness after returning.
  - Conclusion: a 1-week cessation "deload" did not resensitize muscle to hypertrophy and may slightly blunt strength.
  - **Conflict of interest:** funded by Renaissance Periodization.
  - [Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10809978/fullTextXML); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)

### Inferences
- **Default deload policy (inference, practice-based):**
  - **Planned:** schedule one deload week at the end of each mesocycle. Default every **4–6 weeks** (matching the survey mean of 5.6). Let the user choose 3–8.
  - **Content:**
    - Keep the same exercises and session frequency.
    - Cut sets by about **40–60%** (e.g., 4 sets → 2).
    - Raise target RIR by about **2–3** (e.g., RIR 1–2 → RIR 3–5).
    - Either hold load at about 85–100% of normal working load, or reduce it about 10–20% for higher-rep work.
    - Optionally drop accessories.
    - These % values are synthesized from the Delphi and survey direction-of-change data. No study tested specific magnitudes.
  - **Reactive (autoregulated) triggers, any 2 of:**
    - e1RM or reps at a fixed load declining for ≥2 consecutive sessions on a main lift.
    - Readiness or soreness score in the worst decile for ≥3 days.
    - Joint pain flags.
    - Warm-up velocity below baseline (if VBT is used) for ≥2 sessions.
    - When triggered, offer to pull the scheduled deload forward rather than add an extra one.
  - **Avoid prescribing complete rest as a deload** for strength-focused users (Coleman 2024). Allow it as a user choice for life events. Hypertrophy is unlikely to suffer.
  - Same rules for males and females (Delphi consensus against sex-specific deloads).
- Powerlifters approaching a meet: the deload is replaced by the taper (Section 5). Don't stack a deload immediately before a taper.

### Gaps
- No RCT has compared a **reduced-volume/intensity** deload (as opposed to cessation) with continuous training, or compared different deload frequencies or magnitudes. All numeric magnitudes above are inferences. (A targeted search for 2025–2026 deload RCTs was not run; one may exist.)
- Neither the Delphi nor the survey gives % reductions in volume or load.
- Coleman 2024 used training to failure at about 90 sets/wk. Results may differ with lower-effort programs.

## 5. Tapering and peaking for powerlifting (incl. attempt selection)

### Takeaway
**General taper evidence (mostly endurance sports):** a taper of about 2 weeks, with volume reduced about 41–60% and intensity and frequency maintained, is optimal.

**Powerlifters in practice:**
- Peak volume about 5–8 weeks out; peak intensity about 2 weeks out.
- A 7–14 day step (or exponential) taper with volume reduced about 40–60% while intensity is maintained.
- Last heavy (≥90%) singles about 7–10 days out for squat and deadlift, and less than 7 days out for bench.
- Last session 2–4 days before the meet.

Short cessation of 3–4 days appears to preserve or enhance maximal strength. About 5 days may reduce upper-body strength, and ≥1 week off was linked to poor tapers.

**Attempt selection:** openers average about 91–92% of the final best, then about +5% to the second attempt and about +3% to the third. Heavier, successful openers are associated with winning.

### Cited Findings
- **Bosquet et al. 2007, taper meta-analysis** (MSSE 39(8):1358–1365; DOI 10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0; PMID 17762369).
  - 27 studies of competitive athletes, predominantly endurance sports.
  - Overall performance ES 0.59 ± 0.33.
  - The most efficient strategy was a **2-week taper with volume exponentially reduced by 41–60%** and no change to intensity or frequency. Volume reduction was the key driver.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)
- **Pritchard et al. 2016, elite NZ raw powerlifters** (JSCR 30(7):1796–1804; PMID 26670988).
  - 11 elite lifters (Wilks 431.9 ± 43.9), semi-structured interviews.
  - Volume peaked **5.2 ± 1.7 weeks** out. Intensity peaked **1.9 ± 0.8 weeks** out.
  - Taper volume was reduced **58.9 ± 8.4%**, with intensity maintained or slightly reduced.
  - The final session was **3.7 ± 1.6 days** out. Accessories were dropped about 2 weeks out.
  - "Poor tapering occurred when too long (1 week or more) was taken off training."
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26670988/)
- **Grgic & Mikulic 2017, Croatian open-class champions** (JSCR 31(9):2371–2378; PMID 27806009).
  - 10 lifters.
  - Volume reduced **50.5 ± 11.7%** using a step or exponential taper.
  - Intensity maintained or increased, peaking **8 ± 3 days** out.
  - Final-week frequency reduced 47.9 ± 17.5%.
  - Last session **3 ± 1 days** out.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27806009/)
- **Travis et al. 2021, US/Canadian raw powerlifters** (JSCR 35(12S):S26–S35; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000004177; PMID 34846328). Survey of 364 lifters.
  - Highest volume 5–8 weeks out; highest intensity 2 weeks out.
  - Most common taper: a **7–10 day step taper with volume-load reduced 41–50%**. Intensity varied from −30% to +10%.
  - **Final heavy session (>85% 1RM):** 7–10 days out for squat and deadlift, <7 days out for bench, using **90–92.5% 1RM**.
  - The **last session per lift** used 75–80% (squat, bench) and 70–75% (deadlift). Common schemes were 3×2 squat, 3×3 bench and 3×1 deadlift.
  - Cessation before the meet per lift: deadlift 5.8 ± 2.5 d, squat 4.1 ± 1.9 d, bench 3.9 ± 1.8 d. Complete cessation: **2.8 ± 1.1 days**.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34846328/); [ETSU record](https://dc.etsu.edu/etsu-works-2/366)
- **Travis et al. 2020, review** (Sports 8(9):125; PMID 32917000): concludes evidence on peaking maximal strength is limited. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32917000/)
- **Pritchard et al. 2018** (JSCR 32(2):458–465; PMID 29369954).
  - 8 trained men did 4-week blocks, each followed by **3.7 or 5.7 days of cessation**.
  - CMJ (ES 0.30) and isometric bench peak force (ES 0.21) improved over time with no difference between durations.
  - Short cessation can be a taper.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29369954/)
- **Pritchard et al. 2019** (IJSPP 14(4):458–463; PMID 30204523).
  - 11 strength-trained men, crossover design.
  - A taper week with reduced volume at **+5.9% intensity** vs **−8.5% intensity**.
  - No significant difference between tapers. The higher-intensity taper showed small improvements in more measures.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30204523/)
- **Travis et al. 2022** (JSCR; PMID 35180185).
  - 19 strength athletes did a 4-week powerlifting block, then **3 vs 5 days** of cessation.
  - Isometric squat was maintained with both.
  - **Isometric bench decreased after 5 days but not after 3.**
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35180185/)
- **Attempt selection:**
  - **Travis, Zourdos & Bazyler 2021** (Percept Mot Skills 128(1):507–521; PMID 33095691). 109 complete-meet lifters (66 M / 43 F) at the IPF Classic Worlds 2012–2019. Openers were about **91% of the third-attempt weight**, followed by about **+5%** (attempt 1→2) and **+3%** (attempt 2→3). Some sex and lift differences. [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/33095691/)
  - **Howells et al. 2022** (J Sports Med Phys Fitness 62(4):476–484; PMID 33721983). 10,672 Powerlifting Australia squat entries.
    - Openers averaged **92% of the day's best**; 93.5% of lifters went up on later attempts.
    - Winners opened 7 kg heavier (d = 0.14).
    - Third-attempt success: about 68% for winners vs about 64% for non-winners.
    - [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/33721983/)
  - **Darragh et al. 2025** (JSCR; read via search summary only). IPF raw data.
    - Third attempts had lower success than second attempts.
    - For men, 5–20 kg jumps were associated with success. Success declined with jumps >20 kg, especially on third attempts.
    - [DCU repository PDF](https://doras.dcu.ie/31743/1/Darraghetal_2025_PredictingPowerliftingSuccess.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Peaking block generator (inference from survey and practice data; no RCT validates the full sequence).** Example 8–10 week meet prep:
  - **Weeks −10 to −5 (accumulation):** highest volume, moderate intensity (about 70–82%).
  - **Weeks −5 to −2 (intensification):** volume falls about 20–30% per block. Intensity rises to heavy triples, doubles and singles (85–92%+).
  - **Days −10 to −7:** final heavy singles (opener or slightly above, about 90–92.5%) for squat and deadlift. Bench heavy single at about −7 to −5 days.
  - **Final 7–10 days:** step taper. Volume-load −40–60% vs the preceding normal week. Intensity of the top work stays ≥85–90% early, then a last light session per lift (squat/bench 75–80%, deadlift 70–75%, low reps).
  - **Last training day:** 2–4 days before the meet (deadlift last trained about 5–6 days out). Avoid ≥5 days without bench and ≥7 days of total rest.
  - Remove accessories about 2 weeks out.
- **Attempt planner (inference):**
  - Projected best = current e1RM, adjusted for the taper (+0–3%).
  - Opener ≈ **90–92%** of the projected best (heavy but certain; ideally a weight the user has hit for a triple or as an easy single in training).
  - Second ≈ opener +4–6% (about the projected best minus 3%).
  - Third ≈ second +2–4%, up to about 100–102% of projected best, adjusted live by how the second moved (RPE or bar velocity).
  - Warn when a jump exceeds about 20 kg (men) or a proportional amount for lighter lifters.
- Bosquet's endurance-based meta-analysis supports "volume down, intensity up/maintained". The powerlifting-specific numbers are descriptive surveys of what lifters do, not tested optimal values.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares taper durations or volume-reduction percentages in powerlifters using actual 1RM meet performance. The cessation studies used isometric or jump outcomes in small samples (n = 8–19).
- Pritchard et al. 2015 (Strength Cond J review on tapering mechanisms) was not retrieved.
- The Coker et al. finding (a successful first attempt did not raise the odds of a successful third) was seen only as a citation in a search summary and was not verified.
- Taper adjustments for weight cuts and for female lifters (menstrual cycle) were not researched here.

## 6. Detraining and retraining: how fast strength and muscle decline; "muscle memory"; what to do after 1-, 2-, 4-week breaks

### Takeaway
In trained athletes, maximal strength is largely maintained for up to about 2–3 weeks without training. Losses then accelerate (about −14.5% over about 7 weeks in elite football-code athletes). Muscle size loss is slower and smaller in the short term. In older adults it becomes significant only after more than 6 months of cessation. Short planned breaks of 1–3 weeks between blocks do not reduce long-term gains, at least in untrained young men. Retraining regains lost strength faster than it was first gained (older men regained in less than 8 weeks after 12 weeks off). Mechanistic "muscle memory" (retained myonuclei, epigenetic marks) exists, but its functional advantage in humans is modest or inconsistent.

### Cited Findings
- **Bosquet et al. 2013, meta-analysis** (Scand J Med Sci Sports 23(3):e140–e149; DOI 10.1111/sms.12047; PMID 23347054). 103 studies.
  - Effect of training cessation (SMD): submaximal strength −0.62 (−0.80 to −0.45), maximal force −0.46 (−0.54 to −0.37), maximal power −0.20 (−0.28 to −0.13).
  - There was a **dose-response with cessation duration**.
  - Losses were larger in people over 65 and in inactive people than in recreational athletes.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347054/)
- **McMaster et al. 2013, elite rugby and American football** (Sports Med 43(5):367–384; PMID 23529287).
  - **Strength was maintained for up to 3 weeks** of detraining, after which decay accelerated (about 5–16 weeks).
  - Over mean detraining periods of 7.2 ± 5.8 weeks, strength fell **14.5%** (ES −0.64). Power fell 0.4% over 7.6 ± 5.1 weeks.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)
- **Hwang et al. 2017** (JSCR 31(4):869–881; PMID 28328712).
  - 20 resistance-trained men did 4 weeks of training, **2 weeks of detraining**, then 4 weeks of retraining.
  - Leg-press strength stayed elevated with no decrease after detraining. Lean mass was retained.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28328712/)
- **Coleman et al. 2024** (Section 4): 1 week of cessation mid-program gave similar hypertrophy and slightly lower strength gains than continuous training. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)
- **Ogasawara et al. 2013** (Eur J Appl Physiol 113(4):975–985; PMID 23053130).
  - 14 untrained young men did bench press at 75% 1RM, 3×10, 3×/wk.
  - Continuous training for 24 weeks vs periodic training: three 6-week cycles separated by **3-week breaks**.
  - Overall CSA and 1RM gains were similar. Gains during retraining cycles were faster than in the continuous group.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/)
- **Blocquiaux et al. 2020** (Exp Gerontol 133:110860; PMID 32017951). 30 older men.
  - 12 weeks of training: strength and power +10–36%.
  - 12 weeks of detraining: strength and power **−5–15%**. Type II CSA −17%, satellite cells −30% and myonuclei −12% were trends only.
  - Retraining: **under 8 weeks to regain post-training 1RM**.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32017951/)
- **Grgic 2022, meta-analysis in older adults** (IJERPH 19(21):14048; PMID 36360927). 6 studies.
  - Muscle size loss after 12–24 weeks of cessation: d = −0.60 (−1.21 to 0.01), not significant.
  - After 31–52 weeks: d = −1.11 (−1.75 to −0.47).
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36360927/)
- **Encarnação et al. 2022, systematic review** (Muscles 1(1):1). 20 trials.
  - Evidence was insufficient to say how long gains persist. Only 2 studies could be meta-analyzed for strength, and hypertrophy could not be meta-analyzed.
  - Strength gains appeared partly retained after 16–24 weeks of detraining, but the pooled estimates were not significant.
  - [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2813-0413/1/1/1)
- **Cumming et al. 2024** (J Physiol 602(17):4171–4193; PMID 39159314). 12 untrained men and women; unilateral elbow-flexor training for 10 weeks, detraining for 16 weeks, retraining for 10 weeks.
  - **Myonuclei were retained** through detraining: type 2 fibers had 33% more myonuclei than the control arm.
  - **The change in CSA during retraining did not differ** between the previously trained and untrained arm.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39159314/)
- **Seaborne et al. 2018** (Sci Rep 8:1898; PMID 29382913): an epigenetic "memory" of hypertrophy. Some genes stayed hypomethylated after lean mass had returned to baseline, and reloading produced greater hypomethylation. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29382913/)

### Inferences
- **Return-from-break algorithm (inference; % values are synthesized).** Base it on days since the last session that trained the lift, and refine on the first day back with RPE/velocity.

  | Break length | Expected change (evidence) | App action |
  |---|---|---|
  | ≤7 days | No loss; may be a mini-taper (Pritchard 2018; Travis 2022 — but bench may dip slightly after ≥5 days) | Resume the plan. First top set at plan load, autoregulated by RPE. |
  | 8–14 days | Strength maintained in trained men (Hwang 2017) | Session 1 at about 90–95% of the previous working load (or previous e1RM targets) and +1 RIR. Return to plan in 1–2 weeks. |
  | 15–28 days | Strength mostly maintained up to about 3 weeks; small losses at ~4 weeks (McMaster 2013) | Week 1 at about 85–90% load and about 60–70% of usual sets. Week 2 at 90–95%. Re-estimate e1RM from the first sessions' RPE/velocity. |
  | 5–8 weeks | Up to about 10–15% strength loss (−14.5% at ~7 wks) | Ramp over 3–4 weeks from about 75–85% load and about 50% volume. Treat as a new "re-entry" mesocycle. |
  | >2–3 months | Larger losses; retraining fast (older men regained in <8 wks after 12 wks off) | Rebuild e1RMs via submax testing. Expect regained strength in about 4–8 weeks, faster than the original gain. |

- After any break of more than about 10 days, expect higher DOMS for the first 1–2 sessions (Coleman 2024 deload group reported more soreness). Keep volume conservative even if loads feel easy.
- Offer "planned break" support (vacations): breaks of ≤2 weeks need no penalty beyond a soft re-entry. Ogasawara 2013 suggests 3-week breaks every 6 weeks did not reduce 6-month gains in novices.

### Gaps
- Few detraining data exist for **highly trained powerlifters and bodybuilders**. Most evidence comes from elite team-sport athletes, untrained young men or older adults.
- Psilander et al. 2019 (J Appl Physiol, which reportedly found no functional muscle memory in untrained subjects) was not retrieved; I only saw a related letter (Eftestøl et al. 2020).
- No study tests specific load-reduction percentages for returning lifters. The table above is an inference.
- I found no 2024–2026 meta-analysis on detraining in trained young adults.

## 7. Realistic rates of progress (strength and muscle), diminishing returns, sex differences

### Takeaway
**Strength:**
- Powerlifters gain about 7.5–12.5% in their first competitive year and about 20% cumulatively over 10 years (Latella 2024).
- In absolute terms, competitive lifters add about 0.12–0.15 kg/day to their total (≈44–55 kg/yr), and the strongest lifters progress slowest (Latella 2020).
- Elite athletes gain about 7% per year, and gains slow sharply over 2–4 years (McMaster 2013).
- Minimal-dose trainees largely plateau after about 1 year (Steele 2023).

**Muscle:** rate-of-gain figures per month by training age come only from **practitioner heuristics** (McDonald, Aragon).

**Sex differences:** relative hypertrophy is similar between sexes; males gain more in absolute terms. Relative upper-body strength gains favor females.

### Cited Findings
- **Latella et al. 2024** (Sports Med 54(3):753–774; DOI 10.1007/s40279-023-01962-6; PMID 38060089). Longitudinal growth modelling of powerlifting competition data.
  - About **7.5–12.5% strength increase in the first year** and up to about **20% after 10 years**. The largest gains came earliest.
  - Females progressed faster, possibly because of lower baseline strength.
  - Female Masters 3–4 lifters (>59 years) still gained about 2.5–5.0%. Male Masters 4 lifters (>69 years) lost about 0.35%/yr.
  - Limitation: no training-history data.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38060089/)
- **Latella et al. 2020** (JSCR 34(9):2412–2418; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003657; PMID 32865942). Powerlifting Australia 2003–2018: 1,897 lifters (1,271 M / 626 F) with ≥2 meets.
  - Total gain per day: males **0.15 ± 0.44 kg** (≈54.8 kg/yr extrapolated), females **0.12 ± 0.69 kg** (≈43.8 kg/yr). The sex difference was not significant.
  - Males by starting-strength quartile (weakest → strongest): 0.211, 0.137, 0.161, **0.102 kg/day** (≈77 → 37 kg/yr). Q1 vs Q4 differed significantly, suggesting a ceiling effect.
  - First-to-best total improved about 9.5% (M) and 11.4% (F); this is a derived figure.
  - Limitations: training unknown, weak correlations, extrapolated rates.
  - [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7448836/)
- **Steele et al. 2023** (Res Q Exerc Sport 94(4):913–930; DOI 10.1080/02701367.2022.2070592; online 2022). Records from **14,690** people on a minimal-dose program: 1 session/wk, single sets to failure, 6 exercises, up to 352 weeks.
  - Linear-log growth: rapid early gains, then a **plateau after about 1 year** (reported about 30–50% in year 1 and about 50–60% above baseline by year 6).
  - Sex and age had minimal effect on when the plateau emerged.
  - The design is observational.
  - [Journal page](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02701367.2022.2070592); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/69)
- **McMaster et al. 2013** (elite football codes): strength +7.1 ± 1.0% at 12 months, +8.5 ± 3.3% at 24 months, +12.5 ± 6.8% at 48 months. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23529287/)
- **Pelland et al. 2026, dose-response meta-regressions** (Sports Med 56(2):481–505; DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w; PMID 41343037). 67 studies, 2,058 participants.
  - Volume increases both hypertrophy and strength, with **diminishing returns**, more marked for strength.
  - Frequency matters for strength (with diminishing returns) but has negligible effects on hypertrophy.
  - Models were adjusted for training status. Details of volume are out of scope here.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/)
- **Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020** (JSCR 34(5):1448–1460; DOI 10.1519/JSC.0000000000003521; PMID 32218059). Young to middle-aged adults.
  - Relative hypertrophy: ES 0.07 ± 0.06, p = 0.31 (**no sex difference**).
  - Relative upper-body strength: ES −0.60 ± 0.16, p = 0.002, **favoring females**.
  - Lower-body strength: ES −0.21, p = 0.20 (no significant difference).
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)
- **Refalo et al. 2025** (PeerJ 13:e19042; DOI 10.7717/peerj.19042; PMID 40028215). Bayesian meta-analysis of 29 studies, ages 18–45.
  - **Absolute** muscle-size gains favored males: SMD 0.19 (95% HDI 0.11–0.28).
  - **Relative** gains were similar: +0.69% (HDI −1.50 to 2.88%).
  - The male advantage was in the upper body, not the lower body. Training experience did not moderate the results.
  - Earlier preprint versions date to about 2023.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40028215/)
- **Practitioner heuristics (NOT peer-reviewed; label as such in the app):**
  - **Lyle McDonald model, men:** year 1 about 20–25 lb (≈9–11 kg, ≈1 kg/month); year 2 about 10–12 lb; year 3 about 5–6 lb; year 4+ about 2–3 lb/yr. Women about half. Secondary summaries vary.
  - **Alan Aragon model, % of body weight per month:**
    - Beginner: 1–1.5%.
    - Intermediate: 0.5–1%.
    - Advanced (5+ yrs): 0.25–0.5%.
    - Some versions differ.
  - [RNT Fitness summary](https://www.rntfitness.co.uk/realistic-rates-muscle-gain/); [Muscle & Strength summary](https://www.muscleandstrength.com/articles/how-much-muscle-can-you-gain)

### Inferences
- **Expectation bands for the app (inference, anchored to the above).**
  - **Strength, main lifts:**
    - First year of serious training: +10–30% e1RM (novices, especially in the first months, can exceed this). Latella's 7.5–12.5% applies to already-competing lifters.
    - Years 2–4: about +5–10%/yr.
    - 5+ years or near the top quartile: about +2–5%/yr (≈0.5–1 kg/month on a lift).
  - **Muscle (heuristic, flagged as a practitioner model):** display Aragon/McDonald-style ranges, scaled about 50% for women in absolute kg but equal in relative %.
  - **Sex:** use the **same relative (%)** progression targets for men and women. For women, allow equal or faster relative upper-body strength gains early. Apply smaller absolute increments (smaller plates) because absolute loads are lower.
- **Diminishing returns.** Model expected progress per user as a log or exponential-decay curve fitted to their own e1RM history; Latella 2024 and Steele 2023 used growth models of this kind.
  - Flag "plateau" when the e1RM slope over 8–12 weeks is ≤0.
  - Flag "faster than expected" when the slope exceeds about 2× the band. This may be a measurement error, a technique change or novice-like gains.
- **Masters lifters:** strength can still increase into the 60s (females) and decline only slightly in the 70s (males). Reduce expected rates with age instead of assuming decline.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study provides **monthly lean-mass gain rates by training age** in naturally trained lifters. McDonald and Aragon figures are heuristics. Most hypertrophy RCTs last 8–12 weeks and report % change in muscle thickness, not kg of muscle.
- I did not identify which "Steele et al. 2023 meta-analysis of strength/hypertrophy rates" the brief refers to. The Steele RQES 2023 growth-modelling paper is the closest match found.
- Latella 2024 sample sizes and per-lift rates were not retrieved (abstract only).

## 8. Classifying trainee level (novice / intermediate / advanced)

### Takeaway
There is no validated, universally accepted classification.
- ACSM uses training age (novice: untrained or detrained; intermediate: about 6 months; advanced: years).
- Research typically defines "trained" as ≥1 year of consistent resistance training.
- McKay et al. 2022 offer a generic 6-tier participant framework.
- Santos Junior et al. 2021 propose a multi-parameter scored model (beginner → highly advanced), but its cut-offs were not retrieved here.

For an app, a data-driven classification is the most defensible: training age plus the observed rate of e1RM progression, optionally with relative strength.

### Cited Findings
- **ACSM 2009:**
  - Novice: no resistance training, or none for several years.
  - Intermediate: about 6 months of consistent training.
  - Advanced: years of resistance training.
  - Prescriptions and frequencies differ by level (2–3, 3–4 and 4–5 days/wk).
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- **Santos Junior et al. 2021** (Strength Cond J 43(5):77–86).
  - Four levels: beginner, intermediate, advanced and highly advanced. Each level is set by the mean score across several parameters (e.g., training time and strength-related measures).
  - The authors note the field has no objective standard, so misclassification distorts prescription.
  - [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Evaldo-Junior-3/publication/349287497_Classification_and_Determination_Model_of_Resistance_Training_Status/links/6029329b299bf1cc26c4894e/Classification-and-Determination-Model-of-Resistance-Training-Status.pdf)
- **McKay et al. 2022** (IJSPP 17(2):317–331; DOI 10.1123/ijspp.2021-0451; PMID 34965513).
  - Tiers: 0 Sedentary, 1 Recreationally Active, 2 Trained/Developmental, 3 Highly Trained/National, 4 Elite/International, 5 World Class.
  - Based on training volume and performance level. Not specific to resistance training.
  - [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34965513/)
- **Research inclusion conventions:**
  - Plotkin 2022 and Zhang 2023 required ≥1 year of training.
  - Coleman 2024 required ≥1 year of consistent training at ≥3 sessions/wk.
  - [Plotkin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/); [Coleman](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/); [Zhang 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36766912/)
- **Training status moderates outcomes:**
  - Periodization benefits are larger in untrained people (Williams 2017, β = −0.59), while UP > LP emerged only in trained people (Moesgaard 2022).
  - Progress decelerates with training age and baseline strength (Latella 2020, 2024; Steele 2023).
  - [Williams](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/); [Moesgaard](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); [Latella 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7448836/)

### Inferences
- **Proposed app classifier (inference).** Compute a score from:
  - **(a) Consistent training age:** <6 mo, 6–24 mo, 2–5 yr, >5 yr.
  - **(b) Observed progression rate:** e1RM slope on main lifts over the last 8–12 weeks.
    - About ≥1%/week → novice-like.
    - About 0.25–1%/week → intermediate.
    - About <0.25%/week → advanced.
  - **(c) Optional relative strength:** e.g., DOTS/IPF GL points or bodyweight multiples, as a tiebreaker.

  Weight the observed rate most heavily once ≥8 weeks of data exist, because it directly determines the progression model:
  - **Novice:** session-to-session load increases.
  - **Intermediate:** weekly increases or DUP.
  - **Advanced:** block/phase progression with longer cycles and RPE/velocity autoregulation.
- Let users override the label, and re-evaluate it automatically each mesocycle. Users returning after a long break may show "novice-like" rates (retraining) even with a high training age. Do not demote their volume tolerance based only on rate.

### Gaps
- Santos Junior et al. 2021 scoring parameters and cut-offs were not retrieved.
- No validated relative-strength standards (e.g., bodyweight multiples per level) were found in peer-reviewed literature. Commonly used standards tables are practitioner or crowd-sourced.
- The popular practitioner definition (novice progresses session to session, intermediate week to week, advanced month to month; Rippetoe/Kilgore "Practical Programming") was not verified against a source in this session. It is untested empirically.
