# Session- and Exercise-Level Resistance Training Variables: rest intervals, tempo, ROM, exercise selection/variation, exercise-to-muscle mapping, exercise order, advanced set techniques, warm-ups, concurrent training, session length

Scope note for the report writer: these notes cover session-level and exercise-level variables only. Weekly set-volume dose-response, frequency, proximity to failure, 1RM/RPE, periodization/deloads and nutrition belong to other researchers. Where per-session volume or set-counting touches on volume, it is included only because the app needs counting rules. Evidence tags used below: **[MA]** = meta-analysis/systematic review; **[RCT-T]** = trial in resistance-trained people; **[RCT-U]** = trial in untrained or detrained people; **[EMG]** = acute muscle-activation data only (not hypertrophy); **[PP]** = preprint / not peer reviewed; **[PRAC]** = practitioner source, secondary pointer only. Research date: November 2026. Many PMC/publisher full texts were behind bot checks during this session, so several numbers come from PubMed abstracts or search-engine summaries of abstracts. Those are flagged where it matters.

## 1. Rest intervals: how long to rest between sets for hypertrophy vs strength, compounds vs isolation; self-selected rest; volume-load compensation

### Takeaway
For hypertrophy, rests over about 60 s give a small benefit and nothing more shows up beyond about 90 s. The benefit seems to come mostly from keeping volume load (reps × load) up. For maximal strength in trained lifters, rests over 2 min (usually 3–5 min on heavy compounds) work better. Good app defaults: about 2–3 min on compounds and 1–2 min on isolation, with heavy strength sets at 3–5 min, and rest that users can shorten or extend.

### Cited Findings
- **Singer et al. 2024** (Singer A, Wolf M, Generoso L, Arias E, Delcastillo K, Echevarria E, Martinez A, Androulakis Korakakis P, Refalo MC, Swinton PA, Schoenfeld BJ), "Give it a rest: a systematic review with Bayesian meta-analysis on the effect of inter-set rest interval duration on muscle hypertrophy", *Front Sports Act Living* 6:1429789, DOI 10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789, PMID 39205815 [MA]. It pooled 19 measurements (thigh 10, arm 6, whole body 3) from 9 studies. Finding: "a small hypertrophic benefit to employing inter-set rest interval durations >60 s, perhaps mediated by reductions in volume load". It found "no appreciable differences in hypertrophy when resting >90 s", consistent with volume-load penalties levelling off beyond that time-frame. Training to failure or not did not meaningfully change the rest–hypertrophy relationship. Controlled-comparison estimates had wide credible intervals: arm 0.13 (95% CrI −0.27 to 0.51), thigh 0.17 (−0.13 to 0.43), whole body −0.08 (−0.45 to 0.29). Heterogeneity was substantial. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39205815/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2016**, "Longer interset rest periods enhance muscle strength and hypertrophy in resistance-trained men", *J Strength Cond Res* 30(7):1805–1812, PMID 26605807 [RCT-T]. 21 young resistance-trained men were randomized to 1-min (SHORT) or 3-min (LONG) rest. LONG produced significantly greater 1RM gains in squat and bench press and greater ultrasound muscle-thickness gains (anterior thigh significant). Small sample, 8-week program. Rest was not volume-equated, so LONG groups did more volume load. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)
- **Grgic et al. 2017**, "The effects of short versus long inter-set rest intervals in resistance training on measures of muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review", *Eur J Sport Sci* 17(8):983–993, PMID 28641044 [MA, narrative SR]. Six studies (≤60 s vs >60 s). Both short and long rest "may be useful" for hypertrophy. The newer studies in trained participants that used sensitive measures hinted at an advantage for longer rest, but evidence was too sparse to separate the two. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)
- **Grgic et al. 2018**, "Effects of rest interval duration in resistance training on measures of muscular strength: a systematic review", *Sports Med* 48(1):137–151, DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0788-x [MA, SR]. **Untrained:** 60–120 s is enough to maximize strength gains. **Trained:** rests over 2 min are needed to maximize strength gains, although robust strength gains still happen with rests under 60 s. — [VU repository record](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/38537/); [PubMed](https://www.pubmed.gov/28933024/)
- **Older trained-men crossover** (2005, 13 trained men): 2-min and 5-min rest in 3-month blocks produced similar gains in muscle mass and strength. — [PubMed 16095405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16095405/)
- **Longo et al. 2022**, "Volume load rather than resting interval influences muscle hypertrophy during high-intensity resistance training", *J Strength Cond Res* 36(6):1554–1559 [RCT, 80% 1RM]. 1-min vs 3-min rest, each with and without equated volume load. Quadriceps CSA grew more in the groups with greater volume load (LI 13.1%, ES 0.66; volume-load-matched short-rest group VLI-SI 12.9%, ES 0.63) than in the lower-volume-load groups (SI 6.8%, ES 0.38; VSI-LI 6.6%, ES 0.37). The authors conclude that volume load, not rest interval per se, drives hypertrophy. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35622106/)
- **Self-selected rest (acute):** in trained lifters, self-selected rest averaged about 97 s between sets 1 and 2 versus 120 s fixed. Performance and physiological responses were similar, and the authors conclude either approach can be used. This was an acute study, not a hypertrophy trial (*J Funct Morphol Kinesiol* 2024). — [PMC11503322](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11503322/)
- **Self-determined rest, lower body (conference abstract):** compared with fixed 2–3 min, self-determined rest led to longer rests and longer sessions without improving performance or perceived effort. — [IJESAB abstract](https://digitalcommons.wku.edu/ijesab/vol15/iss8/23)
- A 2025 medRxiv preprint by unnamed authors, "Investigating the impact of less than or greater than 60 seconds of inter-set rest on muscle hypertrophy and strength increases in males with >1 year of resistance training experience: systematic review with meta-analysis", exists [PP]. Its full text could not be retrieved (403). — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.22.25336351.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- **App rest-timer defaults** (user-editable). Basis: Grgic 2018 for strength, Singer 2024 for hypertrophy, Schoenfeld 2016 for trained compounds:

  | Context | Default | Allowed range | Rationale |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Powerlifting / strength sets (≤5 reps, ≥~80–85% 1RM), competition lifts & close variants | 3:00 (squat/deadlift 4:00) | 2:30–5:00 | Trained lifters need >2 min to maximize strength (Grgic 2018) |
  | Hypertrophy, multi-joint compounds (squat, press, row, RDL, leg press) | 2:30 | 2:00–3:00 | Larger systemic fatigue; 3-min rest beat 1-min in trained men (Schoenfeld 2016) |
  | Hypertrophy, single-joint / small muscle (curls, raises, extensions, calves) | 1:30 | 1:00–2:00 | Benefit levels off beyond ~90 s (Singer 2024) |
  | Short-rest / time-saving mode | 1:00 | 0:45–1:15 | Allowed. Prompt the user to add about 1 extra set or accept a load drop to keep volume load up (Longo 2022) |
  | Antagonist superset (between A and B) | 0:15–0:30 | 0–1:00 | See Section 7 |
  | Novice (≤6 months training) | 1:30 compounds, 1:00 isolation | — | 60–120 s is enough for strength in untrained people (Grgic 2018) |
- **Volume-load compensation rule:** when rest is ≤60 s and reps on later sets fall by more than about 20–25% versus set 1, show a hint such as "rest longer or add a set". This works because hypertrophy follows volume load more than rest per se (Longo 2022; Singer 2024 mediation). The thresholds are heuristic.
- **Self-selected / auto-rest mode:** offer "ready when ready" with a timer that counts up and a soft minimum (e.g., 60 s isolation / 90 s compound). The acute data show it performs similarly to fixed rest.
- Log actual rest durations. This makes later personalization possible, e.g. "your reps drop X% when rest < Y".

### Gaps
- No direct trial was found that compared rest periods for **isolation vs compound** exercises within the same trained population. The compound/isolation split is extrapolated from fatigue logic plus whole-body trials.
- No longitudinal hypertrophy trial of **self-selected rest** in trained lifters was located. The one 8-week self-paced upper-body study is cited second-hand only.
- The 2025 medRxiv preprint's results could not be retrieved, so its findings are unknown.
- Few rest-interval trials include women or advanced (>5 yr) lifters. Singer 2024 pooled only 9 studies.

## 2. Repetition duration / tempo: rep speed, eccentric emphasis, intentional fast concentric, superslow

### Takeaway
Total rep durations of about 0.5–8 s give similar hypertrophy when sets go to (or near) failure. Very slow reps (>10 s per rep, "superslow") are worse. The best-supported combination is a controlled eccentric of about 2–3 s with a concentric performed with maximal intended velocity. Intended fast concentrics produce greater strength gains than deliberately slowed concentrics.

### Cited Findings
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2015**, "Effect of repetition duration during resistance training on muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Sports Med* 45(4):577–585, DOI 10.1007/s40279-015-0304-0, PMID 25601394 [MA]. Eight studies met inclusion. Hypertrophy was similar across rep durations of 0.5–8 s when training to concentric failure. Training at volitionally very slow durations (>10 s per rep) appears inferior, though sparse controlled research limits firm conclusions. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25601394/); 0.5–8 s summary as cited in [PMC7061915](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7061915)
- **Wilk, Zajac & Tufano 2021**, "The influence of movement tempo during resistance training on muscular strength and hypertrophy responses: a review", *Sports Med* 51(8):1629–1650, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01465-2, PMID 34043184 [narrative review]. Neither isolated slow nor isolated fast tempo is clearly superior for hypertrophy. The most favorable approach appears to be a slower eccentric combined with a faster concentric. Faster concentrics may favor neural/strength adaptations, while longer eccentrics may support hypertrophy without hurting neural adaptations. The chronic effects of individual phase durations had not been isolated. — [PMC8310485](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8310485)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2014**, "Effect of movement velocity during resistance training on neuromuscular performance", *Int J Sports Med* 35(11):916–924 [RCT-T]. 21 resistance-trained young men did 6 weeks of full squat at maximal intended velocity (MaxV, n=10) or deliberately half-maximal velocity (HalfV, n=11) with the same loads. MaxV had a likely more beneficial effect on squat performance. The authors conclude that "movement velocity seemed to be of greater importance than time under tension for inducing strength adaptations". — [Thieme abstract](https://thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/s-0033-1363985?lang=en)
- **González-Badillo et al. 2014** (companion bench-press study, *Eur J Sport Sci*) [RCT]. 20 participants over 6 weeks. MaxV produced significantly greater gains than HalfV in all variables, including 1RM (+18.2% vs +9.7%). These figures come from a search-engine abstract summary. — [repository record](https://rio.upo.es/entities/publication/c94a5051-591d-43bf-99e8-8c11ce0ca9d3/full)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2017**, "Hypertrophic effects of concentric vs. eccentric muscle actions: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *J Strength Cond Res* 31(9):2599–2608, PMID 28486337 [MA]. Eccentric-only actions showed a greater effect size than concentric-only, but the difference was not significant (ES difference 0.25, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.52). Secondary coverage reports mean growth of about 10.0% vs 6.8%. The authors recommend including both actions. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28486337/)

### Inferences
- **Default tempo prescription for the app:** store as an eccentric–pause bottom–concentric–pause top string. Default for hypertrophy work is **"2-0-X-0"** (2 s controlled eccentric, no pause, concentric with maximal intent "X", no pause). Acceptable range for eccentric is 1–4 s. Warn if the user enters a total rep duration over ~8–10 s (superslow is inferior per Schoenfeld 2015).
- **Strength/powerlifting default:** "controlled eccentric, X concentric". Competition-style paused bench (pause on chest) is a sport-specific convention, not an evidence-based hypertrophy requirement. Offer "paused" variants as separate exercises rather than a tempo field.
- **Do not track time-under-tension as a progression metric.** Rep duration within 0.5–8 s doesn't change hypertrophy, and slowing the concentric deliberately reduces strength gains (Pareja-Blanco 2014). Coach cue to display: "lower under control, lift with intent".
- Eccentric-emphasis or tempo-eccentric variants (e.g., 4–5 s eccentric) are fine as optional accessory/technique variations. Eccentric-only overload training is a specialist method and doesn't need a default.
- Novices: the same defaults apply. A slower eccentric (2–3 s) also helps technique consistency (coaching convention, not tested).

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes from Schoenfeld 2015 were not retrieved, and that meta has only 8 studies, few in trained lifters.
- No modern trained-lifter trials were found that isolate eccentric duration (e.g., 2 s vs 4 s) for hypertrophy while holding concentric intent constant. Wilk 2021 notes the same gap.
- Superslow RCTs (e.g., Schuenke 2012, Keeler 2001) were not retrieved this session. The ">10 s inferior" conclusion rests on the 2015 meta.

## 3. Range of motion (ROM), including lengthened partials and training at long muscle lengths

### Takeaway
Full ROM beats partial ROM done mainly in the shortened range, for both strength and (especially lower-limb) hypertrophy. Partials done at long muscle lengths ("lengthened partials") produce hypertrophy similar to, and sometimes greater than, full ROM. Exercises and positions that load muscles at long lengths, such as overhead triceps extension, seated rather than lying leg curl, deep squats for glutes/adductors, and deep-stretch calf raises, have the most consistent support. The idea that long-length training gives *regional* (proximal vs distal) benefits is weakly supported.

### Cited Findings
- **Pallarés et al. 2021**, "Effects of range of motion on resistance training adaptations: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 31(10):1866–1881, DOI 10.1111/sms.14006 [MA, 16 studies]. Full ROM beat partial ROM for strength (ES 0.56, p=0.004) and lower-limb hypertrophy (ES 0.88, p=0.027). There was no significant difference for muscle thickness / pennation angle / fascicle length (ES 0.28, p=0.226) or functional performance (ES 0.44, p=0.186). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34170576/)
- **Wolf et al. 2023**, "Partial vs full range of motion resistance training: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Int J Strength Cond* (2023) [MA]. Main model gave a trivial SMD of 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26) favoring full ROM for hypertrophy. Within it, three studies compared full ROM with partial ROM at long muscle lengths, and those generally favored the lengthened partials. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/99e86b317bec40f5abc2f0008358737e); [Solent record](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/partial-vs-full-range-of-motion-resistance-training-a-systematic-/); subgroup description via [Wolf et al. 2025 SMHS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12869050)
- **Wolf, Androulakis Korakakis, Roberts, Plotkin, Franchi, Contreras, Henselmans, Larsen & Schoenfeld 2025**, "Does longer-muscle length resistance training cause greater longitudinal growth in humans? A systematic review", *Sports Med Health Sci* 8(1):34–42, DOI 10.1016/j.smhs.2025.03.001 [SR, 8 studies, 120 participants, mostly vastus lateralis]. Longer-muscle-length training generally produced more hypertrophy than shorter-length training. Fascicle-length differences were small and equivocal, mostly measured by questionable linear extrapolation. Study quality was poor to fair (SMART-LD 11.4/20). The authors recommend prioritizing long-muscle-length RT for hypertrophy while noting limited evidence. — [PMC12869050](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12869050)
- **Varovic, Wolf, Schoenfeld, Steele et al.**, "Regional hypertrophy with resistance training — does muscle length matter?", SportRxiv preprint 2024 [PP]. A journal version is listed as "Does muscle length influence regional hypertrophy? A systematic review and meta-analysis", DOI 10.1055/a-2615-4935 (Thieme), unverified whether findings changed. Bayesian meta-analysis of 12 studies in young adults. Training at longer mean muscle lengths did not clearly produce greater *regional* hypertrophy: there were small effects favoring longer lengths with substantial uncertainty. Stronger by Science [PRAC] summarizes this as a "very modest, likely trivial" regional effect, partly because the length contrasts in studies were small. — [SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/464); [SBS summary](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/muscle-length-regional-hypertrophy/)
- **Wolf et al. 2025, PeerJ** [RCT-T, within-participant]. In trained individuals, lengthened partial reps produced muscular adaptations similar to full-ROM reps (elbow flexor/extensor thickness and unilateral lat-pulldown 10RM). — [PeerJ 18904](https://peerj.com/articles/18904)
- **Pedrosa et al. 2022**, "Partial range of motion training elicits favorable improvements in muscular adaptations when carried out at long muscle lengths", *Eur J Sport Sci*, PMID 33977835 [RCT-U]. 45 untrained women, knee extension. The partial ROM in the initial (lengthened, knee-flexed) range gave greater relative hypertrophy in some quadriceps regions (distal) than full ROM or shortened partials. Strength gains were similar. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33977835/)
- **Kassiano et al. 2023**, "Greater gastrocnemius muscle hypertrophy after partial range of motion training performed at long muscle lengths", *J Strength Cond Res* 37(9):1746–1753 [RCT-U, young women]. Medial gastrocnemius: initial-ROM (lengthened) partials +15.2% vs full ROM +6.7% vs final-ROM partials +3.4% (p≤0.009). Sources disagree on the lateral gastrocnemius result. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37015016/)
- **Maeo et al. 2021**, "Greater hamstrings muscle hypertrophy but similar damage protection after training at long versus short muscle lengths", *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 53(4):825–837 [RCT-U, within-subject]. 20 adults, 12 weeks, 5 sets × 10 reps at 70% 1RM, twice a week. Seated (hip-flexed) leg curl beat prone leg curl for whole-hamstring MRI volume (+14% vs +9%) and for each biarticular hamstring. The monoarticular short head of biceps femoris grew similarly (+10% vs +9%). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)
- **Maeo et al. 2023**, "Triceps brachii hypertrophy is substantially greater after elbow extension training performed in the overhead versus neutral arm position", *Eur J Sport Sci* 23(7) (epub 2022), DOI 10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279 [RCT-U, within-subject, 12 weeks, cable]. Overhead arm gained about 1.4–1.5× more muscle volume for the long head, the lateral+medial heads, and whole triceps, despite 34–39% lower absolute loads. Strength gains were similar. A prior study (Stasinaki 2018) found no significant difference. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)
- **Kubo, Ikebukuro & Yata 2019**, "Effects of squat training with different depths on lower limb muscle volumes", *Eur J Appl Physiol*, DOI 10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y [RCT-U]. 17 men, full squat (n=8) vs half squat (n=9), 10 weeks, twice a week. Quads grew similarly in both groups. Adductor (p=0.026) and gluteus maximus (p=0.008) volumes grew significantly more with full squats. Hamstrings did not change in either group. Full-squat 1RM rose 31.8% vs 11.3%. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y)
- **Hip angle in leg extension** (*J Sports Sci* 2025, PMID 39699974; preprint DOI 10.51224/SRXIV.407) [RCT-U, within-subject]. 22 untrained men trained one leg at 40° and the other at 90° hip flexion for 10 weeks. The 40° condition (more reclined, so rectus femoris longer) produced greater rectus femoris growth. Vastus lateralis did not differ. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39699974/)

### Inferences
- **Default ROM rule:** "full ROM the joint allows with good technique", with emphasis on reaching the **lengthened (stretched) position** under load. Flag exercises where users habitually shorten ROM at the stretch end (calves, leg extension, triceps overhead, RDL), e.g. cue "get the full stretch".
- **Lengthened partials:** don't make them the default. Offer them as (a) a technique flag on a set, "extend set with lengthened partials after full-ROM failure", or (b) a standalone ROM option for exercises where the shortened range is mechanically easy or unloaded (calf raise, leg extension, cable/DB flyes, curls). Count them as normal sets for volume, since Wolf 2025 PeerJ found similar adaptations.
- **Avoid shortened-range partials** as a default hypertrophy method (Kassiano 2023; Pedrosa 2022; Pallarés 2021). Exception: **powerlifters** may use deliberate partials (board presses, pin squats, block pulls) for overload or sticking-point practice. This is specificity convention and untested for hypertrophy.
- **Exercise-choice preferences driven by muscle length** (strongest evidence first): seated > lying leg curl (Maeo 2021); overhead triceps extension included alongside pressdowns (Maeo 2023); deep squat over half squat for glutes/adductors (Kubo 2019); calf raises with deep dorsiflexion stretch (Kassiano 2023); leg extension with a reclined backrest for rectus femoris (J Sports Sci 2025). Treat region-specific (proximal/distal) claims as low-confidence (Varovic preprint).
- **Powerlifting competition lifts:** ROM is fixed by rules (squat depth, bench touch, deadlift lockout). The app should treat these as fixed-ROM exercises.
- Novices: full ROM is the safe default. Most lengthened-training data come from untrained people (Maeo, Pedrosa, Kassiano, Kubo), and the one trained within-subject trial (Wolf 2025) shows parity, not superiority.

### Gaps
- Most long-length studies are in **untrained** people, often women (Pedrosa, Kassiano) or young men (Maeo). Trained-lifter evidence is limited to Wolf 2025 PeerJ.
- No trial was located that compares **lengthened partials appended after failure** (a common bodybuilding usage) against simply doing another set.
- Pooled estimates from the peer-reviewed Thieme version of the regional meta-analysis were not retrieved, because Thieme returned a bot check.
- Other 2025 studies mentioned only in search summaries (quadriceps knee-flexion ROM with no effect; shoulder-flexed vs extended curls with no regional difference) could not be verified and are not cited as findings.

## 4. Exercise selection & variation: rotating exercises, regional (non-uniform) hypertrophy, multi-joint vs single-joint

### Takeaway
Using more than one exercise per muscle, chosen to cover different regions and muscle lengths, gives more complete (more uniform) hypertrophy than a single exercise. Changing exercises at random every session doesn't improve hypertrophy and may cost some strength-specific gains, though it boosts motivation. Single-joint and multi-joint exercises give similar whole-muscle hypertrophy, and adding single-joint work to a good multi-joint program usually adds little for trained people. Exceptions are muscles that multi-joint lifts don't load well (e.g., rectus femoris, biarticular hamstrings, triceps long head, biceps relative to rows).

### Cited Findings
- **Kassiano et al. 2022**, "Does varying resistance exercises promote superior muscle hypertrophy and strength gains? A systematic review", *J Strength Cond Res* 36(6):1753–1762, PMID 35438660 [SR]. "Some degree of systematic variation seems to enhance regional hypertrophic adaptations and maximize dynamic strength, whereas excessive, random variation may compromise muscular gains." The evidence base was small and all young men. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/); evidence-base description via [repositorio.usp.br](https://repositorio.usp.br/item/003221381)
- **Kassiano et al. 2024/2025**, "Muscle hypertrophy and strength adaptations to systematically varying resistance exercises", *Res Q Exerc Sport* 96(2):371–381, DOI 10.1080/02701367.2024.2409961 [RCT-U]. 70 young women, constant (n=38) vs varied (n=32) exercises, 3×/week for 10 weeks. Strength gains and hypertrophy were "virtually similar". — [repositorio.usp.br](https://repositorio.usp.br/item/003221381)
- **Baz-Valle et al. 2019**, "The effects of exercise variation in muscle thickness, maximal strength and motivation in resistance trained men", *PLoS One* 14(12):e0226989 [RCT-T]. 21 trained men, 8 weeks, 4×/week, 3 sets × 6 exercises. Exercises were either fixed or randomly varied each session by an app. Bench/squat 1RM and VL/RF thickness gains were similar. Vastus intermedius increased significantly only in the fixed group, with no between-group difference. Intrinsic motivation improved moderately and significantly only in the varied group. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881066/)
- **Fonseca et al. 2014**, "Changes in exercises are more effective than in loading schemes to improve muscle strength", *J Strength Cond Res* 28(11):3085–3092 [RCT, physically active]. 12 weeks, twice a week. Groups that varied exercises (squat, leg press, deadlift, lunge) showed hypertrophy in all quadriceps heads. Some squat-only groups did not show vastus medialis and rectus femoris hypertrophy, and varied-exercise groups gained more strength. — [BVS record (PMID 24832974)](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-24832974)
- **Costa et al. 2021**, "Does performing different resistance exercises for the same muscle group induce non-homogeneous hypertrophy?", *Int J Sports Med*, DOI 10.1055/a-1308-3674 [RCT, detrained]. 22 detrained men, same vs varied exercises, 3×/week for 9 weeks, thickness measured proximal/mid/distal on thigh, elbow flexors and extensors. The varied group gained at all sites measured. — [Thieme PDF](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/a-1308-3674.pdf)
- **Zabaleta-Korta et al. 2021**, "The role of exercise selection in regional muscle hypertrophy: a randomized controlled trial", *J Sports Sci* 39(20):2298–2304. Squat vs leg extension, with rectus femoris and vastus lateralis measured proximal/central/distal. Conclusion: exercise selection plays a role in regional hypertrophy. Critics note the different resistance profiles confound exercise choice with muscle length. — [letter & response, PubMed 35291926](https://www.pubmed.gov/35291926/); follow-up on resistance profiles (recreationally trained women) [PMC10407320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10407320)
- **Burke et al. 2024** (*J Sci Sport Exerc*): exercise selection can influence regional hypertrophy of lower-limb muscles, measurable within 8 weeks. — [JSSE](https://journal.hep.com.cn/josisae/EN/10.1007/s42978-024-00299-4)
- **Ema et al. 2016**, "Unique activation of the quadriceps femoris during single- and multi-joint exercises", *Eur J Appl Physiol*, DOI 10.1007/s00421-016-3363-5 [EMG]. Rectus femoris activation was higher in knee extension than in leg press, with vasti not different. RF activation during the multi-joint exercise was low and did not rise with fatigue. — [Springer Medicine abstract](https://www.springermedicine.com/unique-activation-of-the-quadriceps-femoris-during-single-and-mu/21072252)
- **Rosa et al. 2023**, "Hypertrophic effects of single- versus multi-joint exercise of the limb muscles: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Strength Cond J* 45:49–57 [MA, 7 studies / 10 comparisons]. Trivial pooled difference, so whole-muscle hypertrophy was similar between SJ and MJ. SJ may allow more selective targeting of muscle subdivisions, which is a hypothesis. — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/45635/)
- **Gentil et al. 2015**, "Single vs. multi-joint resistance exercises: effects on muscle strength and hypertrophy", *Asian J Sports Med* 6(2):e24057 [RCT-U]. 29 untrained men did lat pulldown only or biceps curl only for 10 weeks. Elbow-flexor size and strength gains were similar. Volume was not equated. — [PMC4592763](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4592763)
- **Mannarino et al. 2021**, "Single-joint exercise results in higher hypertrophy of elbow flexors than multijoint exercise", *J Strength Cond Res* 35(10) [RCT-U, within-subject, n=10, 8 weeks]. Biceps curl arm +11.06% vs dumbbell-row arm +5.16% elbow-flexor hypertrophy. — [PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Single-Joint%20Exercise%20Results%20in%20Higher%20Hypertrophy%20of%20Elbow%20Flexors%20Than%20Multijoint%20Exercise.pdf)
- **Barbalho et al. 2020**, "Single joint exercises do not provide benefits in performance and anthropometric changes in recreational bodybuilders", *Eur J Sport Sci* 20(1):72–79 [RCT-T]. MJ-only vs MJ+SJ showed no added benefit from SJ. — [Solent record](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/single-joint-exercises-do-not-provide-benefits-in-performance-and/)
- **de França et al. 2015** (*Appl Physiol Nutr Metab*) [RCT-T]. 20 men with ≥2 years RT experience: adding SJ to MJ produced no significant difference in any variable. — [PubMed 26244600](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26244600/) (as summarized in [Paoli 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.01105/full))
- **Paoli et al. 2017**, "Resistance training with single vs. multi-joint exercises at equal total load volume: effects on body composition, cardiorespiratory fitness, and muscle strength", *Front Physiol* 8:1105 [RCT, physically active men]. At equal volume load, MJ programs gave greater strength and VO2max gains, with no body-composition difference. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fphys.2017.01105/full)
- **Bourne et al. 2017**, "Impact of the Nordic hamstring and hip extension exercises on hamstring architecture and morphology", *Br J Sports Med* 51:469–477 [RCT, recreationally active men, n=30, 10 weeks, MRI]. 45° hip extension increased biceps femoris long head volume more than Nordics (d=1.03, p=0.037). Semitendinosus grew with both, not significantly different (d=0.69, p=0.239). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27660368/)

### Inferences
- **Rotation policy (app default):** keep each muscle's exercise selection **fixed within a mesocycle** (e.g., 4–8 weeks) so load progression can be tracked. Rotate 1–2 exercises per muscle between mesocycles ("systematic variation"). Offer "random variation" only as an opt-in "variety mode" for motivation (Baz-Valle 2019). Warn that excessive random variation may compromise gains (Kassiano 2022).
- **Coverage rule for hypertrophy templates:** for each target muscle, choose exercises that together (a) load the muscle at long lengths and (b) cover its sub-regions/heads. Suggested pairings:
  - Quads: squat/leg press (vasti) **plus** leg extension (rectus femoris; Ema 2016 EMG, Zabaleta-Korta 2021, hip-angle trial 2025).
  - Hamstrings: hip-hinge (RDL/45° hip extension; BFLH per Bourne 2017) **plus** knee-flexion curl, preferably seated (Maeo 2021). Squats don't count for hamstrings (Kubo 2019).
  - Triceps: an overhead extension (long head; Maeo 2023) **plus** pressdown or pressing.
  - Elbow flexors: rows/pulldowns are not a full replacement for curls (Mannarino 2021, about half the growth). Include at least one curl for hypertrophy goals.
  - Glutes: deep squat or hip thrust (similar glute hypertrophy, Plotkin 2023, see Section 5).
  - Chest: flat **plus** incline press/fly if upper-chest emphasis is desired (EMG and single-trial support only, see Section 5).
- **Powerlifters:** competition lifts (or close variants) stay in every block for specificity. Variation goes into accessory slots. Multi-joint lifts first for strength (Paoli 2017; also Section 6).
- **Novices:** a small fixed menu of multi-joint lifts plus a few isolation lifts is enough. Variation matters little early on (Kassiano 2024 women; Gentil 2015).

### Gaps
- Only one trial in trained men tested random variation (Baz-Valle 2019, n=21, 8 weeks). The "excessive variation compromises gains" claim in Kassiano 2022 is based on very few studies.
- No trained-lifter trial was found that defines an **optimal rotation interval** (every session vs every 2 weeks vs every mesocycle).
- Zabaleta-Korta 2021 sample size and effect sizes were not retrieved.
- Single-joint vs multi-joint evidence is almost entirely limb muscles, mainly the elbow flexors.

## 5. Exercise-to-muscle mapping for volume attribution (primary/secondary muscles, fractional set credit)

### Takeaway
The best-validated set-counting scheme for hypertrophy credits a set as **1.0 for muscles trained directly and 0.5 for muscles trained indirectly** ("fractional" method). Few direct hypertrophy (MRI/ultrasound) studies exist per exercise. The best evidenced are: squat → quads, glutes, adductors with **~0 hamstrings**; bench press → pecs > triceps; rows → elbow flexors at about half the stimulus of curls; hip thrust ≈ squat for glutes; seated curl and overhead triceps extension favor biarticular heads. Everything else is convention or EMG-based and should be marked as such.

### Cited Findings
- **Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos**, "The resistance training dose-response: meta-regressions exploring the effects of weekly volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and strength gain", SportRxiv preprint (v2, 2024) [PP/MA, 67 studies in preprint]. Sets were scored direct = 1, indirect = 0.5 ("fractional"), compared with "total" (indirect = 1) and "direct-only" (indirect = 0). Relative evidence was strongest for the **fractional** method, which was used for the primary models. — [SportRxiv 460](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460). A *Sports Medicine* 2026 version is listed in a catalogue (unverified details). — [sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)
- **Remmert, Robinson, Pelland et al.** (per-session volume, SportRxiv preprint) [PP]. Fractional vs direct counting must be distinguished to quantify dose-response. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **Squat:** quads increased similarly with full or half squats. Glute max and adductors grew more with full squats. **No hamstring change** in either group (Kubo 2019, MRI). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00421-019-04181-y)
- **Hip thrust vs back squat** (Plotkin et al. 2023, *Front Physiol*, DOI 10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170) [RCT-U, 9 weeks, set-volume equated, HT n=18 vs SQ n=16, MRI]. Gluteus maximus hypertrophy was similar between groups; estimates modestly favored HT, with CIs crossing zero. The squat group had greater thigh (quad/adductor) hypertrophy. Hip thrust showed higher glute EMG, but EMG did not predict hypertrophy. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)
- **Adding hip thrust** to leg press + stiff-leg deadlift raised gluteus maximus growth (+9.3% vs +6.0%, p=0.016) in 33 untrained women. This is a preprint-style report, and individual exercise contributions couldn't be separated [PP]. — [Research Square](https://researchsquare.com/article/rs-2882506/v1)
- **Bench press:** in a 24-week time-course study in 7 previously untrained men, pectoralis major thickness rose from week 1 while triceps rose only after week 5, so the chest responds earlier and more than the triceps. Search summary of abstract. — [PubMed 24265879](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24265879/)
- **Incline vs flat bench (regional pecs):** high-density EMG showed the greatest amplitudes at the sternocostal head for flat bench and the clavicular head for 45° incline [EMG]. — [PubMed 34644424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34644424/). A training study (47 untrained men; horizontal vs incline vs combined) reportedly found the greatest upper-pec (2nd intercostal space) thickness gain with incline. That study was seen only via search summary (possibly [PubMed 36334406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334406/)) and needs verification.
- **Rows/pulldowns → elbow flexors:** dumbbell row produced about 47% of the elbow-flexor growth of curls (5.16% vs 11.06%; Mannarino 2021) — [PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Single-Joint%20Exercise%20Results%20in%20Higher%20Hypertrophy%20of%20Elbow%20Flexors%20Than%20Multijoint%20Exercise.pdf). Lat pulldown alone produced elbow-flexor growth similar to curls alone in untrained men (not volume-equated; Gentil 2015). — [PMC4592763](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4592763)
- **Latissimus dorsi:** no published MRI hypertrophy comparison of pulldown vs row was found. A registered trial (NCT07360236, ~20 participants, unilateral pulldown vs row, MRI of lats, biceps, brachialis, posterior deltoid, teres major; start 2026-01) has no results yet. — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07360236)
- **Leg press / multi-joint → rectus femoris:** RF activation is low in leg press and doesn't increase with fatigue (Ema 2016 [EMG]). Knee extension at a reclined hip angle grows RF more (J Sports Sci 2025). — [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/unique-activation-of-the-quadriceps-femoris-during-single-and-mu/21072252); [PubMed 39699974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39699974/)
- **Hip extension (45° back extension) → hamstrings:** more BFLH growth than Nordics; semitendinosus grew with both (Bourne 2017). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27660368/)
- **Leg curl → hamstrings:** seated > prone for the biarticular heads (Maeo 2021). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)
- **Overhead vs neutral elbow extension → triceps:** overhead gave about 1.4–1.5× more growth in all heads (Maeo 2023). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)

### Inferences

- **Implementation notes:** (1) Store the mapping as data, not code: per exercise a list of {muscle, fraction, evidence_tag, source_id}, editable by advanced users. (2) Report weekly volume **two ways**: "direct sets" (fraction = 1 only) and "fractional sets" (sum of fractions). The validated default display is the fractional total (Pelland preprint). (3) Use a sub-muscle taxonomy where evidence supports regional effects: quads → vasti vs rectus femoris; hamstrings → biarticular vs short head; triceps → long vs lateral/medial; pecs → clavicular vs sternocostal; gastrocnemius vs soleus. Roll these up to parent muscles for display. (4) Show the evidence tag in the UI, e.g. "squat does not count toward hamstrings (MRI evidence)".

- No hypertrophy (MRI/ultrasound) trials were found for: lats with pulldown vs row (trial pending), deltoid heads with overhead press vs lateral raise, deadlift variants, dips, spinal erectors. These rows are convention.
- The bench-press triceps contribution is supported only by small, older untrained time-course data. Exact percent changes were not retrieved because PubMed blocked fetching.
- The 0.5 indirect weight is a population-level modelling choice. It wasn't derived per exercise, and finer weights (0.25/0.75) have no validation.
- Whether the Sports Medicine 2026 version of Pelland et al. changed the fractional-method conclusion was not verified.

## 6. Exercise order within a session

### Takeaway
Exercise order **doesn't meaningfully affect hypertrophy**, but **strength gains are larger for exercises done earlier in the session**. Order by priority: put the lifts whose strength you most want to improve (competition lifts for powerlifters; lagging muscles for bodybuilders) first. Multi-joint before single-joint is a sensible default, not a rule.

### Cited Findings
- **Nunes et al. 2021**, "What influence does resistance exercise order have on muscular strength gains and muscle hypertrophy? A systematic review and meta-analysis", *Eur J Sport Sci* 21(2):149–157 (online 2020), PMID 32077380 [MA, 11 studies, about 268 participants per secondary summary]. Strength increases were largest in exercises performed at the beginning of a session. No significant order effect on hypertrophy (ES = 0.03, p = 0.862). MJ→SJ and SJ→MJ may give similar results. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)
- **Simão et al. 2012**, "Exercise order in resistance training", *Sports Med* 42:251–265 [narrative review]. Placing an exercise early in the session raises repetitions over multiple sets, and therefore volume, for that exercise. Order should not always follow "large before small muscle groups". It should be driven by client needs and priority movements. The pre-exhaustion method may not increase recruitment of the larger muscle. Summary from abstract plus secondary commentary. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.2165%2F11597240-000000000-00000)
- **Pre-exhaustion trials:** Fisher et al. 2014 (*Appl Physiol Nutr Metab*, 39 trained participants, 12 weeks) found no strength or body-composition benefit of pre-exhaustion over MJ-first order — [PubMed 25092528](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25092528/). Trindade et al. 2019 (*Front Physiol*) found pre-exhaustion gave strength gains similar to traditional order — [PMC6882301](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6882301/)

### Inferences
- **Default ordering algorithm** for auto-generated sessions:
  1. Priority/specificity lifts first: competition lifts for powerlifters; user-flagged "priority muscle" exercises for bodybuilders.
  2. Then multi-joint, heaviest/most technical first (e.g., squat/deadlift before leg press, barbell before machine).
  3. Then single-joint/isolation.
  4. Optional conditioning/cardio last (see Section 9).
  Allow drag-and-drop override without warning for hypertrophy goals, because hypertrophy is unaffected by order (Nunes 2021). Show a soft warning only if a user's tracked **strength-goal lift** is moved behind other work for the same muscle.
- **Pre-exhaust:** allow it as a user choice and label it "no proven benefit" (Fisher 2014; Trindade 2019). Count volume normally.
- Within a superset or circuit, order rules apply to each pair's first exercise.
- Novices: same defaults. Order matters little for size, and putting the skills being learned first is sensible (convention).

### Gaps
- Most order studies are short and in untrained/recreational people. Few cover powerlifting-style programs with competition lifts.
- Nunes 2021 subgroup data (e.g., by training status) were not retrieved.

## 7. Advanced set techniques (drop sets, supersets/paired sets, rest-pause, cluster sets, myo-reps, pre-exhaustion, BFR) and how to count them as volume

### Takeaway
Drop sets, supersets, rest-pause and cluster sets give **similar hypertrophy (and broadly similar strength) to traditional sets** when volume is comparable. Their main benefit is **time efficiency**: drop sets and supersets can cut session time by about half to two-thirds. Supersets may slightly blunt strength on heavy multi-joint lifts, rest-pause may slightly help strength, and blood flow restriction (BFR) with low loads matches high-load hypertrophy but not strength. No study has validated how to count these as "sets". The app needs an explicit, documented heuristic.

### Cited Findings
- **Drop sets, Sødal, Kristiansen, Larsen & van den Tillaar 2023**, "Effects of drop sets on skeletal muscle hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Sports Med Open* 9, DOI 10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5 [MA, 6 studies, 5 pooled]. Both drop-set and traditional groups increased hypertrophy, with **no significant between-group difference**. Some drop-set protocols took **one-half to one-third of the time** of traditional training. — [PMC10390395](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10390395/)
- **Drop sets, Coleman et al. 2022**, "Muscular adaptations in drop set vs. traditional training: a meta-analysis", *Int J Strength Cond* [MA, 5 RCTs ≥6 weeks]. Similar effects on strength and hypertrophy, and drop sets may save time. — [VU repository](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/46296/)
- **Drop sets, newer meta (Sports Med Open 2026)** [MA]. Drop sets produce significantly higher perceived exertion, blood lactate and neuromuscular fatigue than traditional training. Its chronic conclusions were not retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-026-01012-1)
- **Drop sets and regional quadriceps:** a leg-extension drop-set trial suggested a potential benefit for non-uniform rectus femoris hypertrophy, with no advantage for vastus lateralis or strength. — [PubMed 34564324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34564324/)
- **Supersets, Zhang, Weakley, Li, Li & García-Ramos 2025**, "Superset versus traditional resistance training prescriptions: a systematic review and meta-analysis exploring acute and chronic effects on mechanical, metabolic, and perceptual variables", *Sports Med*, DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02176-8 [MA, 19 studies, 313 participants]. Supersets shorten sessions without lowering training volume, muscle activation or perceived recovery. Chronic gains in maximal strength and strength endurance were similar. **Agonist–antagonist** pairings allowed more reps than traditional sets (SMD = 0.68, p = 0.01). Supersets may raise perceived exertion and possibly muscle damage. — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/)
- **Supersets, Iversen et al. 2024**, "Efficacy of supersets versus traditional sets in whole-body multiple-joint resistance training: a randomized controlled trial", *J Strength Cond Res* 38(8):1372–1378 [RCT, 10 weeks, twice a week; leg press, bench, lat pulldown, seated row]. Supersets "hampered maximal strength gains somewhat" (notably the pulldown). Muscle-mass and fat-mass changes were similar. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39072654/)
- **Rest-pause, Prestes et al. 2019**, "Strength and muscular adaptations after 6 weeks of rest-pause vs. traditional multiple-sets resistance training in trained subjects", *J Strength Cond Res* [RCT-T, n=18]. Strength gains were similar. Rest-pause gave greater thigh thickness gain (~11% vs ~1%) and more leg-press reps-to-failure gain (~27% vs ~8%). — [PubMed 28617715](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28617715/)
- **Rest-pause & drop sets, Enes et al. 2021**, "Rest-pause and drop-set training elicit similar strength and hypertrophy adaptations compared with traditional sets in resistance-trained males", *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 46(11):1417–1424 [RCT-T, n=28, 8 weeks, twice a week, volume equated]. Hypertrophy was similar across all three. Rest-pause showed slightly superior strength improvements. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34260860/)
- **Cluster sets, Davies et al. 2021**, *Sports Med* 51(4):707–736, DOI 10.1007/s40279-020-01408-3 [MA, 29 studies]. Cluster vs traditional sets: hypertrophy ES −0.05 (95% CI −0.32 to 0.23), strength ES −0.05 (−0.21 to 0.11). There were no differences by volume, cluster model, training status, body part or exercise type. — [PubMed 33475986](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33475986/)
- **Set structures, Jukic et al. 2021** (*Sports Med*) [MA]. Traditional, cluster and rest-redistribution structures did not differ for strength (SMD −0.06) or hypertrophy (SMD −0.03). — [Maastricht record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-set-structure-manipulation-on-chronic-adaptations-/)
- **Myo-reps (protocol, practitioner):** Myo-reps were devised by Borge Fagerli. One near-failure "activation" set (roughly 1–2 RIR) is followed by mini-sets at the same load, separated by **3–5 deep breaths** (about 5–15 s in some descriptions, up to 20–40 s in others). Typically 3–5 mini-sets, stopping when target reps can't be hit. Mini-set size is often about 25% of activation reps (e.g., 3–5 reps). These brackets are coaching conventions [PRAC]. — [Barbell Medicine](https://www.barbellmedicine.com/blog/myo-reps/); [StrengthLog](https://www.strengthlog.com/?p=36118)
- **Myo-reps (evidence):** an 8-week Liberty University dissertation (n=8 trained men) reported comparable hypertrophy and strength to traditional sets with shorter session time [thesis, very small n] — [Liberty DC](https://digitalcommons.liberty.edu/doctoral/6303). A trial in trained men reported by practitioners (Biolayne; Henselmans) found similar strength and size gains with myo-reps using about 29% fewer reps in about 62% less time (90-s rests between exercises, 1–2 RIR). It was described as **still in peer review** at the time of reporting, and Henselmans notes it was not an equivalence design [PRAC/PP]. — [Biolayne](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-50/myo-reps-for-mass/); [Henselmans](https://mennohenselmans.com/3-epic-new-studies-to-build-more-muscle-2026/)
- **Pre-exhaustion:** no benefit for strength or body composition in 39 trained participants (Fisher 2014) — [PubMed 25092528](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25092528/). Similar strength to traditional (Trindade 2019) — [PMC6882301](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6882301/)
- **BFR, Lixandrão et al. 2018**, "Magnitude of muscle strength and mass adaptations between high-load resistance training versus low-load resistance training associated with blood-flow restriction: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Sports Med* 48(2):361–378, DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0795-y [MA]. High-load (>65% 1RM) RT gives greater **strength** gains, while low-load (<50% 1RM) BFR-RT and high-load RT appear **equally effective for muscle mass**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0795-y); summary via [PMC11595635](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11595635/)
- **Time efficiency:** Iversen et al. 2021 conclude supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time while maintaining volume, and are probably better suited to hypertrophy than to maximal strength. — [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)

### Inferences
- **When the app should suggest each technique:**
  - *Supersets (antagonist pairs, e.g., row + bench, curl + triceps):* default suggestion when the user enables "time-saver" or sets a session time cap. Avoid pairing two heavy multi-joint **strength-priority** lifts for powerlifters (Iversen 2024 found blunted strength). Non-competing pairs, such as upper + lower or isolation + isolation, are fine. Suggested rest: 0–30 s between A and B, then the normal rest (Section 1) after B.
  - *Drop sets:* last set of isolation or machine exercises for hypertrophy goals and time saving. Not on competition lifts. Typical drop 20–30% load, 1–2 drops. The magnitudes are convention and were not established by the metas retrieved.
  - *Rest-pause / myo-reps:* isolation or machine exercises, moderate loads (e.g., activation set of 12–20 reps), hypertrophy blocks, time-limited sessions.
  - *Cluster sets:* heavy strength/power work, e.g. powerlifting singles/doubles, to keep bar speed up. Not a hypertrophy tool. Equivalent outcomes (Davies 2021) mean they can be used purely for quality/comfort.
  - *Pre-exhaust:* permitted, no proven benefit. Don't auto-suggest.
  - *BFR:* optional for users with joint pain or deloads, or for low-load accessory work (20–40% 1RM, typical protocol conventions). Expect hypertrophy similar to high-load but less strength (Lixandrão 2018). The app shouldn't prescribe cuff pressures. Show a safety note to use proper equipment.
- **Volume-counting rules (heuristic; no validated standard exists).** Proposed defaults, documented in-app and user-adjustable:

  | Technique | Proposed set credit | Rationale |
  |---|---|---|
  | Superset / paired set / circuit | Each exercise's sets count normally (1.0 each, then the muscle fractions from Section 5) | Volume is unchanged by pairing (Zhang 2025) |
  | Pre-exhaust | Count normally | Just order + short rest |
  | Drop set | Main set 1.0 + **0.5 per drop**, capped at **2.0** per drop-set sequence | Drop-set protocols matched multi-set hypertrophy in a fraction of the time (Sødal 2023; Coleman 2022), but each drop has fewer reps at lighter load |
  | Rest-pause (e.g., set to ~failure + 2–3 mini-sets after 10–20 s) | Activation 1.0 + **0.33–0.5 per mini-set**, capped at **2.0** | Rest-pause ≈ traditional hypertrophy at equal volume (Enes 2021; Prestes 2019) |
  | Myo-reps (activation + 3–5 mini-sets of 3–5 reps) | Activation 1.0 + **0.33 per mini-set**, capped at **2.5** (≈ 2–3 traditional sets) | Practitioner equivalence claims; unpublished trial shows similar gains with fewer reps |
  | Cluster set (e.g., 4 × 2 reps with 20–30 s intra-set rest) | **1.0 per cluster block** (i.e., as many sets as the traditional configuration with the same total reps) | Equal outcomes vs traditional sets (Davies 2021; Jukic 2021) |
  | Lengthened partials (standalone sets) | 1.0 per set | Similar adaptations to full ROM in trained lifters (Wolf 2025 PeerJ) |
  | Lengthened partials appended after failure | +0.25 to that set (optional), capped at 1.25 | Untested. Treat as minor extra stimulus |
  | BFR set taken near failure | 1.0 per set | Similar hypertrophy to high-load (Lixandrão 2018) |
  | Warm-up / ramp sets | 0 | Not training sets (Section 8) |
- Store technique type as a set-level attribute (`set_type`: normal | warmup | drop | rest_pause_mini | myo_activation | myo_mini | cluster_rep_block | partial_extension | bfr). Volume credit is then computed from configuration rather than hard-coded, so rules can be updated as evidence changes.
- Novices: generally don't suggest advanced techniques in the first ~3–6 months. Traditional sets work fine, and these techniques raise perceived exertion and fatigue (2026 drop-set meta; Zhang 2025). This is convention.

### Gaps
- **No study validates how to convert drop sets, rest-pause or myo-reps into "set equivalents".** All proposed credits are heuristics.
- The Krzysztofik et al. 2019 advanced-techniques review (*IJERPH*) and the Jukic cluster-set meta full texts were not retrieved.
- The myo-reps randomized trial in trained men was unpublished or in peer review at the time of reporting. Its sample size differs between sources (22 vs 24).
- Prestes 2019 rest-pause protocol details (mini-set rest, rep targets) and drop-set load-reduction magnitudes across trials were not retrieved.
- BFR details (cuff pressure, % occlusion) weren't researched, since they are out of scope for a tracker.

## 8. Warm-ups: general vs specific warm-up, stretching, and a warm-up ramp generator for a working weight

### Takeaway
A **specific warm-up** (progressive sets of the same exercise) is the key element before heavy lifting. Warm-ups that use only a few reps at very light loads aren't enough. A ramp that reaches about 80% of the working load helps velocity and power output. A short **general warm-up** (low-moderate cardio) on top can raise 1RM. **Static stretching of ≥60 s per muscle** right before lifting causes small strength/power decrements, while short stretches followed by dynamic activity are harmless.

### Cited Findings
- **Ribeiro et al. 2020**, "The role of specific warm-up during bench press and squat exercises: a novel approach", *Int J Environ Res Public Health* 17(18):6882, PMID 32971729 [RCT-T crossover, 40 resistance-trained men]. Training was 3 × 6 at 80% 1RM. Warm-ups compared: WU = 6 reps at 40% + 6 reps at 80% *of the training load*; WU80 = 6 reps at 80% of the training load; WU40 = 6 reps at 40%. **Squat:** WU80 gave higher mean propulsive velocity than WU40 in sets 2–3 (ES 0.51–0.80). **Bench:** the progressive WU shortened time-to-peak-velocity versus WU40 and increased work. Conclusion: "warming-up with few repetitions and low loads is not enough to optimize squat and bench press performances". — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32971729/)
- **Ribeiro et al. 2021** (*J Men's Health*) [34 men]: a 2 × 6 warm-up at 40% and 80% of the training load raised first-set movement velocity in bench press and squat. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/90de36e35e514c36af5f61118a42eb3a)
- **Ribeiro et al. 2014**, "Effect of different warm-up procedures on the performance of resistance training exercises", *Percept Mot Skills* [RCT]. Repetition performance in resistance exercises was not influenced by warm-up type. — [BL record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100170261683_0x000001)
- **Ribeiro et al. 2021 systematic review** (*Motricidade*): results on general + specific warm-up were "not consensual". Some studies found specific warm-up no different from none, and others found specific-only warm-up best for maximal strength. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/25ec6f05b30f409c83052eb328c0d9f7)
- **Abad et al. 2011**, "Combination of general and specific warm-ups improves leg-press one repetition maximum compared with specific warm-up in trained individuals", *J Strength Cond Res* 25(8):2242–2245 [crossover, n=13 trained]. Adding 20 min of cycling at 60% HRmax before the same specific warm-up raised leg-press 1RM by 8.4% on average (p=0.002). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21544000/)
- **Barroso et al. 2013** (16 strength-trained men): a long-duration, low-intensity general warm-up gave leg-press 1RM about 3% higher than other general warm-up intensity/duration combinations. — [QxMD/PubMed 22692116](https://read.qxmd.com/read/22692116/the-effects-of-different-intensities-and-durations-of-the-general-warm-up-on-leg-press-1rm)
- **McCrary, Ackermann & Halaki 2015**, "A systematic review of the effects of upper body warm-up on performance and injury", *Br J Sports Med* 49(14):935–942, PMID 25694615 [SR, 31 RCTs]. Strong evidence that **high-load dynamic warm-ups improve power and strength**. Short static stretching had no effect on power. No injury-prevention data. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25694615/)
- **Behm, Blazevich, Kay & McHugh 2016**, "Acute effects of muscle stretching on physical performance, range of motion, and injury incidence in healthy active individuals: a systematic review", *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 41(1):1–11, PMID 26642915 [SR]. Immediate performance changes: static stretching about −3.7%, PNF about −4.4%, dynamic stretching about +1.3%. Deficits were larger with holds ≥60 s per muscle group. ROM gains usually lasted under 30 min. They recommend including stretching within a warm-up that has post-stretch dynamic activity. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26642915/)
- **Kay & Blazevich 2012** [SR]: detrimental effects of static stretch are mainly limited to durations ≥60 s. — [PubMed 21659901](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21659901/)
- **Iversen et al. 2021:** for time efficiency, restrict warm-up to exercise-specific warm-ups, and prioritize stretching only if the goal is flexibility. — [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772)

### Inferences
- **Warm-up generator (proposed algorithm, heuristic).** It is informed by Ribeiro 2020 (ramp reaching ~80% of working load), McCrary 2015 (high-load dynamic warm-up), and the principle of not inducing fatigue. Inputs: `working_weight W`, `target_reps R`, `is_first_exercise_for_pattern`, `bar_weight`, `plate_increment`.
  - **Rule 0 – general warm-up (optional prompt at session start):** 5–10 min easy cardio or dynamic movement. Abad 2011 used 20 min at 60% HRmax and found +8.4% 1RM, but 5–10 min is a time-efficient convention.
  - **Rule 1 – heavy/strength sets (R ≤ 5 or W ≥ ~80% e1RM):**
    - empty bar × 8–10 (if barbell)
    - 40% W × 5
    - 60% W × 3
    - 75% W × 2
    - 85% W × 1
    - add 92–95% W × 1 if W ≥ ~90% e1RM or if it is a 1RM attempt day
  - **Rule 2 – hypertrophy compounds (R 6–15):**
    - (bar × 8–10 if barbell)
    - 40–50% W × 6–8
    - 70% W × 4
    - 85% W × 2 (optional; drop if W is light in absolute terms)
    This corresponds to Ribeiro's 6 @ 40% + 6 @ 80%, with reps tapered to limit fatigue.
  - **Rule 3 – isolation exercises or a muscle already warmed by an earlier exercise:** 1 set × 8–12 at ~50% W, or skip. For the second and later exercises on the same pattern: 1 "feeder" set × 3–5 at ~70% W.
  - **Rounding & pruning:** round each warm-up load to the nearest available increment; drop steps below bar weight or within ~5% of the previous step; never prescribe warm-up reps ≥ working reps at loads ≥70% W.
  - **Rest between warm-up sets:** 30–60 s for the light steps, 1–2 min before the first work set after the ≥75% steps (convention).
  - **Volume:** warm-up sets are flagged `set_type = warmup` and **excluded from volume and progression metrics**.
- **Stretching guidance:** default to no pre-lift static stretching. If the user wants it, cap holds at <60 s per muscle and follow with dynamic movement (Behm 2016; Kay & Blazevich 2012). Mobility drills for squat depth, for example, are fine.
- Novices: use the same ramp. The lighter steps double as technique practice.

### Gaps
- No trial has directly compared multi-step ramp protocols (e.g., 4–6 steps at 40–95%) for heavy powerlifting singles/triples. The Rule 1 ramp is coaching convention extrapolated from Ribeiro 2020 and McCrary 2015.
- Warm-up evidence is acute performance only. No study links warm-up structure to long-term hypertrophy or strength.
- Injury-prevention effects of resistance-training warm-ups are undocumented (McCrary 2015 found no studies).

## 9. Concurrent training (cardio + lifting): interference with hypertrophy and maximal strength; modality, timing/separation, amount

### Takeaway
In current meta-analyses, adding aerobic training **doesn't meaningfully impair whole-muscle hypertrophy or maximal strength** on average. The exceptions are: (a) **explosive strength/power**, which is blunted especially when both are done in the same session; (b) **lower-body maximal strength in trained individuals (and in men)** when both are done in the same session; and (c) possibly a small reduction in fiber-level hypertrophy. For lifters, the practical rules are: separate cardio from lower-body lifting by ≥6 h (ideally different days); lift before cardio if in the same session; prefer low-impact or cycling for steady-state work; and keep endurance volume moderate. Older evidence links interference to higher frequency and duration and to running.

### Cited Findings
- **Wilson et al. 2012**, "Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises", *J Strength Cond Res* 26(8):2293–2307, PMID 22002517 [MA, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes]. Concurrent training with **running, but not cycling**, gave significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Hypertrophy ES: strength-only 1.23 vs concurrent 0.85. Endurance **frequency and duration** correlated negatively with strength/power outcomes (r = −0.26 to −0.75). The authors conclude that interference depends on modality, frequency and duration. — [record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1027372546); [BVS](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/sms/resource/pt/mdl-22002517)
- **Schumann et al. 2022**, "Compatibility of concurrent aerobic and strength training for skeletal muscle size and function: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis", *Sports Med* 52(3):601–612, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7 [MA, 43 studies]. Concurrent training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development". **Explosive strength gains may be attenuated, especially when aerobic and strength training are performed in the same session.** Results appeared independent of aerobic type, concurrent frequency, training status and age. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)
- **Fiber-level companion meta-analysis from the Schumann group (2022)**, *Sports Med* 52:2391–2403 (first author believed to be Lundberg TR; authorship not verified this session) [MA]. Concurrent training may have a **small negative effect on muscle-fiber hypertrophy** compared with strength training alone. Running showed a larger effect on type I fibers than cycling. Its authors note that none of the individual studies reporting whole-muscle interference used the most reliable size measures. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-022-01688-x)
- **Petré et al. 2021**, "Development of maximal dynamic strength during concurrent resistance and endurance training in untrained, moderately trained, and trained individuals: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Sports Med* 51:991–1010, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01426-9 [MA]. Concurrent training impaired **lower-body 1RM (leg press/squat) development in trained** individuals but not in moderately trained or untrained ones. The negative effect in trained people occurred **only when both were done in the same session**. The authors suggest separating sessions when maximal strength is the priority. — [PMC8053170](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8053170/)
- **Huiberts et al. 2024**, "Concurrent strength and endurance training: a systematic review and meta-analysis on the impact of sex and training status", *Sports Med* 54(2):485–503, DOI 10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9 [MA, 59 studies, 1,346 participants]. Lower-body strength was blunted in **men** (SMD −0.43, 95% CI −0.64 to −0.22) but not women (0.08, −0.34 to 0.49; group difference p=0.03). There were no differences between trained and untrained for strength outcomes. More data are needed in highly strength-trained athletes. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01943-9)
- **Murlasits et al. 2018**, "The physiological effects of concurrent strength and endurance training sequence: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *J Sports Sci*, PMID 28783467 [MA]. Lower-body 1RM was higher when **strength preceded endurance** (+3.96 kg, 95% CI 0.81–7.10). Sequence had no effect on aerobic capacity. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- **Eddens et al. 2018**, "The role of intra-session exercise sequence in the interference effect: a systematic review with meta-analysis", *Sports Med* [MA, 10 studies]. Resistance-then-endurance improved lower-body dynamic strength (+6.91%, 95% CI 1.96–11.87). No effect on static strength or hypertrophy. — [PubMed 28917030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28917030/)
- **Sabag et al. 2018**, "The compatibility of concurrent high intensity interval training and resistance training for muscular strength and hypertrophy: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *J Sports Sci* [MA]. HIIT + RT gave similar hypertrophy and upper-body strength, but less lower-body strength gain (ES −0.248, p=0.049). Subgroups: cycling HIIT −0.377 (p=0.074) vs running HIIT −0.176 (p=0.261). The authors suggest running-based HIIT and longer rest between modalities. — [PubMed 29658408](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29658408/)
- **Robineau et al. 2016**, "Specific training effects of concurrent aerobic and strength exercises depend on recovery duration", *J Strength Cond Res* [RCT, 58 amateur rugby players, 7 weeks, 0 vs 6 vs 24 h between sessions]. Strength interference depended on the recovery delay. 0 h, and to a lesser extent 6 h, were not optimal. VO2peak improved more with 24 h. — [PubMed 25546450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25546450/)

### Inferences
- **Scheduling rules for the planner** (soft warnings, not hard blocks):
  1. **Same-day cardio + lower-body lifting:** warn if the gap is <6 h. Suggest ≥6 h, ideally a different day or after upper-body sessions (Petré 2021; Robineau 2016; Schumann 2022 for explosive strength).
  2. **Same session:** order **lifting first, then cardio** by default (Murlasits 2018; Eddens 2018).
  3. **Modality default for steady-state cardio:** cycling, incline walking, rowing or elliptical rather than running when hypertrophy/strength is the priority. This follows Wilson 2012 and the 2022 fiber-level meta on type I fibers. **Note the conflict:** Schumann 2022 found no modality effect, and for HIIT, Sabag 2018 found a *more negative* trend for cycling than running. So for HIIT, don't prefer cycling; just keep it away from heavy leg days.
  4. **Amount:** interference scales with endurance frequency and duration (Wilson 2012). Default soft caps for lifters whose primary goal is strength or hypertrophy: about 2–3 moderate-intensity sessions per week of ≤30–45 min, plus unrestricted low-intensity activity (walking). These numbers are heuristic and not drawn from a dose-response trial in this search.
  5. **Powerlifters in peaking/strength blocks:** stronger warning for HIIT or long runs within 24 h before heavy lower-body sessions (Petré 2021 trained same-session effect; Sabag 2018 lower-body strength; Schumann 2022 explosive strength).
  6. **Sex:** interference on lower-body strength appeared in men, not women (Huiberts 2024). The app could relax warnings for female users, but data in highly trained women are sparse.
- Track cardio sessions (modality, duration, intensity zone) as separate session types so the scheduler can compute time gaps to the nearest lower-body lifting session.
- Novices: interference is negligible on average (Petré 2021: no effect in untrained or moderately trained). Use relaxed warnings.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis focused on **highly trained bodybuilders/powerlifters**. Huiberts 2024 explicitly calls for this.
- Wilson 2012's exact frequency/duration thresholds (often quoted second-hand as about >3 days/week or >20–30 min/day) were not verified in the full text, so they aren't stated as findings.
- The optimal separation between 6 h and 24 h hasn't been tested in strength-trained lifters. Robineau 2016 used rugby players.
- Interference with **hypertrophy specifically** in trained lifters is uncertain. Whole-muscle metas show none, but there is a small fiber-level effect.

## 10. Session duration, per-session set caps and time-efficient training

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy returns per session diminish, with no detectable extra benefit beyond about **11 fractional sets per muscle per session**. For strength, the corresponding point is about **2 direct sets per session** of the tested lift (preprint). Session time can be roughly halved with supersets, drop sets and rest-pause without lowering volume. Bilateral multi-joint exercises and exercise-specific warm-ups are the most time-efficient choices.

### Cited Findings
- **Remmert, Robinson, Pelland et al.** (per-session set-volume meta-regression), SportRxiv preprint [PP]. Per-session volume shows a positive dose-response with diminishing returns for strength and hypertrophy. The "point of undetectable outcome superiority" is about **2 direct sets for strength** and about **11 fractional sets for hypertrophy** per session. Strength returns diminish more strongly. There is insufficient data at very high per-session volumes. — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [FAU press release](https://fau.edu/newsdesk/articles/build-muscle-gain-strength-study.php)
- **Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld & Fimland 2021**, "No time to lift? Designing time-efficient training programs for strength and hypertrophy: a narrative review", *Sports Med* 51:2079–2095, DOI 10.1007/s40279-021-01490-1 [narrative review, mostly untrained/recreational samples]. Prioritize bilateral, multi-joint exercises with full dynamic ROM. Supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve training time while maintaining volume. Warm-ups should be exercise-specific, with stretching only for flexibility goals. — [PMC8449772](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772); critique by Carlson, Smith & Fisher 2022 (athletes may also need time efficiency): [letter](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/629394/1/Letter%20to%20Editor%20Iverson_FINAL_after%20revisions.pdf)
- Supersets reduce session duration without reducing volume (Zhang 2025) — [PMC12011898](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12011898/). Drop-set protocols took one-half to one-third the time of traditional sets (Sødal 2023) — [PMC10390395](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10390395/)

### Inferences
- **Per-session soft cap:** warn when **fractional sets for any single muscle in one session exceed ~10–12**, e.g. "additional sets for this muscle today likely add little; consider moving them to another day". This is based on the Remmert preprint. Treat it as a soft nudge, because it is a preprint and the uncertainty at high volumes is large.
- **Session-duration estimator** for planning UI: `duration ≈ Σ_sets (reps × rep_duration[~3–4 s default] + rest_after_set) + warm-up time [~1 min per warm-up set] + transitions [~1–2 min per exercise]`. Use logged rest times once available.
- **"Time-cap mode":** when the user's planned session exceeds their time budget, the app proposes in order: (1) convert antagonist / non-competing exercise pairs to supersets; (2) convert final isolation sets to drop sets or myo-reps (credited per Section 7); (3) trim warm-ups to Rule 2/3 minimums; (4) shorten isolation rest to 60–90 s with a volume-load note. Never shorten rest on strength-priority competition lifts below 2 min.
- Powerlifters: per-session direct sets on a competition lift beyond about 2 add little measurable strength in the preprint model. However, the volume dose-response belongs to the other researchers, so present this only as a planning hint.

### Gaps
- The per-session meta-regression is a **preprint**. Its peer-reviewed status and final estimates should be rechecked.
- No trial directly tests an optimal **session duration in minutes** for trained lifters. Duration effects are always confounded with volume and rest.
- Iversen 2021 targets the general public. Its applicability to competitive bodybuilders and powerlifters is limited, as its critics note.
