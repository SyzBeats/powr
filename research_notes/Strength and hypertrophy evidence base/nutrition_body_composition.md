# Nutrition and Body-Composition Science for Muscle Growth and Strength (Bodybuilders and Powerlifters): Implementation-Ready Evidence Notes

Scope: protein, energy balance for bulking and cutting, energy-expenditure equations, adaptive TDEE, bodyweight trend smoothing, body-fat and FFMI estimation, powerlifting weight cuts, supplements, nutrient timing, sex differences, and safety thresholds. Out of scope: training variables, 1RM/RPE formulas, and powerlifting scoring.
Units: kcal = 4.184 kJ. BW = body weight (body mass), FFM = fat-free mass, LBM = lean body mass.
Evidence labels used below: [META] systematic review/meta-analysis; [RCT]; [POS] position stand/consensus; [NARR] narrative review/opinion; [OBS] survey/observational; [MODEL] mathematical model; [PRACT] practitioner/non-peer-reviewed.
Verification note: "verified" means the number was read from the abstract or full text during this session. Anything marked UNVERIFIED comes from widely reproduced standard formulas that I could not check against a primary text this session. Verify those before shipping.

## Q1. Protein: daily targets, needs in an energy deficit, per-meal dosing and distribution, pre-sleep protein

### Takeaway
Default protein target: about 1.6 g/kg/day, the Morton 2018 breakpoint, with about 2.2 g/kg/day as a "maximize" upper target. In a deficit, lean trained lifters should go higher: 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM (Helms 2014), or about 1.8–2.7 g/kg BW (Roberts 2020). Spread intake across 3–6 meals of about 0.4 (0.3–0.55) g/kg each. Total daily protein matters more than timing. The 2023 Trommelen tracer study weakens the idea of a strict per-meal "ceiling" of 20–25 g.

### Cited Findings
**Daily intake: dose-response meta-analyses**
- [META] Morton RW et al. 2018, *Br J Sports Med* 52(6):376–384, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608, PMID 28698222. 49 RCTs, n=1,863, resistance training (RT) ≥6 wk with protein supplementation. Supplementation increased 1RM strength by 2.49 kg (95% CI 0.64–4.33), FFM by 0.30 kg (0.09–0.52), fibre CSA by 310 µm² (51–570), and mid-femur CSA by 7.2 mm² (0.20–14.30). Each year of age reduced FFM gains by 0.01 kg. Resistance-trained participants gained more FFM: +0.75 kg (0.09–1.40), p=0.03. Breakpoint: "1.62 g/kg/day," beyond which there were no further FFM gains. An erratum was published in 2020 (PMID 32943392). — [PubMed 28698222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)
- The upper bound of about 2.2 g/kg/day for "maximizing" comes from the upper confidence limit of the Morton breakpoint. Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018 use 2.2 g/kg/day as the upper daily intake, and Slater 2019 cites guidelines of 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day. — [Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/); [Slater et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)
- [META] Nunes EA et al. 2022, *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 13(2):795–810, doi:10.1002/jcsm.12922, PMID 35187864. 74 RCTs in healthy, non-obese adults. With RT, protein increased lean body mass (LBM) with SMD 0.22 (95% CI 0.14–0.30; 62 studies), lower-body strength with SMD 0.40 (the CI is printed as 0.09–0.35, which is likely a typo in the source), and bench press with SMD 0.18 (0.03–0.33). Lean-mass benefit appeared at 1.2–1.59 g/kg/day in adults ≥65 y and at ≥1.6 g/kg/day in adults <65 y. — [PubMed 35187864](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35187864/)
- [META] Tagawa R et al., *Nutr Rev* 2021;79(1):66–75 (epub 2020), doi:10.1093/nutrit/nuaa104, PMID 33300582. 105 articles, n=5,402, spline dose-response model. Below 1.3 g/kg/day, each +0.1 g/kg/day of protein added +0.39 kg LBM (95% CI 0.36–0.41). Above 1.3 g/kg/day the gain was +0.12 kg (0.11–0.14). The range studied was 0.5–3.5 g/kg/day over several months. — [PubMed 33300582](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33300582/)
- [META] Tagawa R et al. 2022, *Sports Med Open* 8:110, doi:10.1186/s40798-022-00508-w, PMID 36057893. With RT, protein increased strength by 2.01% (1.09–2.93). Strength rose about 0.72% (0.40–1.04) per +0.1 g/kg/day up to 1.5 g/kg/day, with no further gain above that. Several authors are Meiji Co. employees (COI). — [PubMed 36057893](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36057893/)
- [POS] Jäger R et al. 2017, ISSN position stand on protein and exercise, *JISSN* 14:20, doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8, PMID 28642676. Recommendations: 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day for building and maintaining muscle; 2.3–3.1 g/kg/day during hypocaloric periods in resistance-trained people; >3.0 g/kg/day may benefit body composition; 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g per dose, with 700–3,000 mg leucine, every 3–4 h; 30–40 g casein pre-sleep. The anabolic effect of exercise lasts "at least 24 h." — [PubMed 28642676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- [POS] IOC consensus (Maughan 2018), protein: 1.6 g/kg/day described as optimal, with up to 2.2 g/kg/day tolerated without adverse effects; 0.3–0.5 g/kg per meal, 3–4 times daily. Effects on lean mass with RT are "not large." — [Maughan et al. 2018, PMC5867441](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/)

**Energy deficit and contest prep**
- [META/SR] Helms ER, Zinn C, Rowlands DS, Brown SR 2014, *Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab* 24(2):127–138, doi:10.1123/ijsnem.2013-0054, PMID 24092765. 6 studies, 13 groups. Inclusion: resistance-trained for more than 6 months, energy-restricted, body fat ≤23% (men) or ≤35% (women). FFM fell 0.3–2.7 kg in 9 of 13 groups. Estimated need is about 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM, scaled upward with greater caloric restriction and greater leanness. — [PubMed 24092765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)
- [NARR] Helms, Aragon, Fitschen 2014, *JISSN* 11:20, doi:10.1186/1550-2783-11-20, PMID 24864135, natural bodybuilding contest prep. Recommendations: protein 2.3–3.1 g/kg LBM/day; fat 15–30% of kcal, with carbohydrate as the remainder; 3–6 meals/day; 0.4–0.5 g/kg protein per meal before and after training. — [PubMed 24864135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)
- [NARR] Roberts BM, Helms ER, Trexler ET, Fitschen PJ 2020, *J Hum Kinet* 71:79–108, doi:10.2478/hukin-2019-0096, PMID 32148575. Physique athletes, both sexes: protein 1.8–2.7 g/kg; 4–5 protein feedings/day, including one near training and one before sleep. — [PubMed 32148575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148575/)
- [NARR/opinion] Ribeiro AS, Nunes JP, Schoenfeld BJ 2019, *Sports Med* 49(10):1481–1485, doi:10.1007/s40279-019-01111-y. Competitive bodybuilders often eat more protein than the 1.4–2.0 g/kg guidelines and "may benefit" from higher intakes, but direct research in this group is limited. — [PubMed 31028659](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31028659/)

**Per-meal dose, distribution, and the "ceiling"**
- [NARR] Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA 2018, *JISSN* 15:10, doi:10.1186/s12970-018-0215-1, PMID 29497353. The commonly cited per-meal ceiling is about 20–25 g, but not all protein above 20 g is oxidized. Recommendation: at least 0.4 g/kg/meal across at least 4 meals to reach at least 1.6 g/kg/day. At the 2.2 g/kg/day upper end, that means up to 0.55 g/kg/meal. — [PubMed 29497353](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/)
- [Tracer study] Trommelen J et al. 2023, *Cell Rep Med* 4(12):101324, doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2023.101324, PMID 38118410. Quadruple-isotope study after whole-body resistance exercise. 100 g protein produced a "greater and more prolonged (>12 h) anabolic response" than 25 g. — [PubMed 38118410](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/)

**Timing and safety**
- [META] Schoenfeld, Aragon, Krieger 2013, *JISSN* 10:53, doi:10.1186/1550-2783-10-53, PMID 24299050. Data: 23 hypertrophy studies (n=525) and 20 strength studies (n=478). Once covariates were included, protein timing had no significant effect. Total protein intake was the strongest predictor of hypertrophy. — [PubMed 24299050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/)
- [META] Devries MC et al. 2018, *J Nutr* 148(11):1760–1775, doi:10.1093/jn/nxy197, PMID 30383278. 28 studies, n=1,358, comparing higher-protein diets (≥1.5 g/kg, ≥20% energy, or ≥100 g/day) with lower intakes. Post-intervention GFR: SMD 0.19 (trivial, higher in the high-protein groups). Change in GFR: SMD 0.11, not significant. No evidence of harm to kidney function in healthy adults. — [PubMed 30383278](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30383278/)
- [NARR] Slater 2019 states there was no renal effect at 1.8 g/kg/day and that health effects above about 2.5 g/kg/day have not been validated. Protein above 3.0 g/kg may raise diet-induced thermogenesis by about 500 kJ/day (about 120 kcal) compared with 1.0 g/kg. — [Slater et al. 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)

### Inferences
- Suggested app defaults:
  - Maintenance or bulk: target = 1.6 g/kg BW/day, with a configurable range of 1.6–2.2.
  - Cut: target = 2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM when FFM is known. Otherwise use 1.8–2.7 g/kg BW, scaling upward with leanness and deficit size.
  - In obese users, use FFM or a goal/adjusted weight. Per-kg BW targets overshoot when fat mass is high; Helms' FFM-based figures were designed for lean athletes.
- Per-meal guidance: `perMeal_g = clamp(0.4 × BW, 0.3 × BW, 0.55 × BW)`. Suggest `meals = ceil(dailyTarget / perMeal)`, bounded to 3–6. Optionally suggest a 30–40 g casein-rich pre-sleep feed. Present distribution as secondary to the daily total (Schoenfeld 2013).
- Evidence quality: daily-intake targets rest on large meta-analyses (high confidence for averages). Deficit targets rest on 6 small studies (Helms 2014) plus expert reviews (moderate-to-low confidence). Per-meal targets are mechanistic and acute-MPS-based (moderate confidence).

### Gaps
- The Morton breakpoint 95% CI (often cited as 1.03–2.20 g/kg/day) is not in the abstract and I did not verify it from the full text this session.
- Trommelen 2023 sample size and population details were not captured.
- There are no long-term RCTs in competitive bodybuilders at >2.5 g/kg/day showing extra hypertrophy (Ribeiro 2019).
- Female-specific protein dose-response data were not found.

## Q2. Energy surplus and rate of weight gain for lean mass ("bulking")

### Takeaway
Use a small surplus: about 5–20% above maintenance, or about 350–500 kcal/day as a starting point. Target gain of about 0.25–0.5% BW/week for novice and intermediate lifters, and lower for advanced lifters. Larger surpluses mainly add fat. In trained lifters, a 15% surplus did not beat a 5% surplus for muscle thickness (Helms 2023).

### Cited Findings
- [NARR] Iraki J, Fitschen P, Espinar S, Helms E 2019, *Sports (Basel)* 7(7):154, doi:10.3390/sports7070154, PMID 31247944. Off-season natural bodybuilders. Recommendations:
  - Energy surplus about 10–20%.
  - Weight gain about 0.25–0.5% BW/week for novice and intermediate lifters; advanced lifters should be more conservative.
  - Protein 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day at 0.40–0.55 g/kg/meal across 3–6 meals.
  - Fat 0.5–1.5 g/kg/day.
  - Carbohydrate at least 3–5 g/kg/day.
  - Supplements: creatine 3–5 g/day, caffeine 5–6 mg/kg, beta-alanine 3–5 g/day, citrulline malate 8 g/day.
  — [PubMed 31247944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31247944/)
- [RCT] Helms ER et al. 2023, *Sports Med Open* 9:102, doi:10.1186/s40798-023-00651-y, PMID 37914977. Design: 21 randomized, 17 completed; trained lifters; 8 weeks of RT 3×/week; maintenance vs +5% vs +15% energy; Bayesian analysis.
  - No evidence of group differences in muscle thickness or squat 1RM.
  - Bench 1RM improved more in the +15% group than in the +5% group (BF10=9.9) and the maintenance group (BF10=14.5).
  - Skinfolds increased more in the +15% group than in the maintenance group (BF10=4.2).
  - Bodyweight change predicted skinfold change (R²=0.49) and only weakly predicted biceps thickness change (R²=0.24).
  - Small sample: COVID-19 reduced completers.
  — [PubMed 37914977](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37914977/)
- [RCT] Garthe I et al. 2013, *Eur J Sport Sci* 13(3):295–303, doi:10.1080/17461391.2011.643923, PMID 23679146. Design: elite athletes (n=39); 8–12 weeks; 4 strength sessions/week; nutrition counselling for a surplus vs ad libitum eating.
  - Intake: 3,585±601 vs 2,964±884 kcal.
  - BW gain: +3.9% vs +1.5%.
  - Fat mass: +15% vs +3%.
  - Lean mass gain: no difference between groups. 1RM rose 6–12% in both.
  - 40 m sprint declined in the surplus group only.
  — [PubMed 23679146](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23679146/)
- [NARR] Slater GJ et al. 2019, "Is an energy surplus required to maximize skeletal muscle hypertrophy associated with resistance training," *Front Nutr* 6:131, doi:10.3389/fnut.2019.00131, PMID 31482093. The surplus size needed is unknown.
  - Textbook guidance is about 1,500–2,000 kJ/day (about 360–480 kcal). An extra about 4,000 kJ/day (about 950 kcal) is suggested for "hard gainers" or very heavy training loads.
  - The authors' practical advice: start at about 1,500–2,000 kJ/day and monitor body composition and strength.
  - Energy stored in 1 kg of muscle is about 5,000–5,200 kJ (about 1,200–1,240 kcal). Including synthesis costs, estimates are about 7,440 kJ/kg skeletal muscle (Forbes; about 1,780 kcal) or 6,050 kJ/kg (Joosen and Westerterp; about 1,450 kcal).
  - Overfeeding without RT (twin study, about 4,200 kJ/day for 100 days) produced about 2 kg fat per 1 kg lean.
  - In a pilot study in male bodybuilders, an aggressive intake of about 282 kJ/kg/day (about 67 kcal/kg/day) gave larger body-mass and muscle gains, with methodological concerns. This appears to be Ribeiro et al. 2019, *J Hum Kinet*; the summary did not name it.
  - Less-trained or younger lifters have greater hypertrophy potential, so they can justify larger surpluses.
  - Carbohydrate 4–7 g/kg/day for strength athletes. Fat 20–35% of energy and not below 15–20%. Cutting fat from 33.3% to 13.9% of energy lowered resting testosterone.
  — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)

### Inferences
- App logic for a bulk:
  - `targetRate_%BW_per_wk`: novice 0.5, intermediate 0.25–0.5, advanced 0.1–0.25. The novice and intermediate values come from Iraki 2019. The advanced range is my reading of "more conservative."
  - Initial surplus: `kcal_surplus = rate × BW_kg × ρ_gain / 7`, where ρ_gain is the energy cost per kg gained. ρ_gain is unknown for trained lifters. A plausible working value is about 5,000–7,000 kcal/kg for mixed lean and fat gain. Example: 80 kg × 0.25%/wk = 0.2 kg/wk, which needs about 140–200 kcal/day.
  - Then adjust from the observed weight trend (see Q4/Q5) rather than trusting ρ.
  - Cap the surplus suggestion at about 20% of TDEE.
- Warn when the weekly trend exceeds about 0.5% BW/week (novice) or about 0.25–0.35% BW/week (advanced) for 3 or more weeks. Helms 2023 and Garthe 2013 suggest the excess is mostly fat.
- Evidence quality: low to moderate. There is one small RCT in trained lifters (Helms 2023) and one RCT in elite athletes who were not bodybuilders (Garthe 2013). The rest is expert opinion.

### Gaps
- No RCT defines an optimal surplus or gain rate by training status in trained bodybuilders or powerlifters. Female-specific bulking data are lacking.
- I did not retrieve full details of the Ribeiro 2019 *J Hum Kinet* bodybuilder pilot (exact intakes and outcomes).

## Q3. Energy deficit and cutting: rate of loss, lean-mass and strength retention, diet breaks and refeeds, macronutrient distribution

### Takeaway
Target loss of 0.5–1.0% BW/week (Helms 2014), with slower rates near contest leanness. In athletes, about 0.7%/week preserved lean mass and strength better than about 1.4%/week (Garthe 2011). Deficits of about 500 kcal/day or more largely block lean-mass gains during RT, while strength gains are relatively preserved (Murphy & Koehler 2022). In resistance-trained adults, diet breaks (ICECAP) did not improve body composition but did reduce hunger.

### Cited Findings
- [NARR] Helms, Aragon, Fitschen 2014, *JISSN* 11:20. Target BW loss of about 0.5–1%/week. Protein 2.3–3.1 g/kg LBM. Fat 15–30% of kcal. Carbohydrate is the remainder. — [PubMed 24864135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864135/)
- [RCT] Garthe I, Raastad T, Refsnes PE, Koivisto A, Sundgot-Borgen J 2011, *IJSNEM* 21(2):97–104, doi:10.1123/ijsnem.21.2.97, PMID 21558571. Design: elite athletes, n=24, 4 RT sessions/week, slow loss target (0.7%/week) vs fast loss target (1.4%/week).
  - Energy intake cut by 19% vs 30%. Achieved loss 0.7±0.8 vs 1.0±0.4%/week. Both groups lost about 5.5% BW.
  - Duration: 8.5±2.2 vs 5.3±0.9 weeks.
  - LBM: +2.1±0.4% (slow) vs −0.2±0.7% (fast); between-group p<.01.
  — [PubMed 21558571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/)
- [RCT follow-up] Garthe I, Raastad T, Sundgot-Borgen J 2011, *IJSNEM* 21(5):426–435, doi:10.1123/ijsnem.21.5.426, PMID 21896944. Completers: n=14 vs 9.
  - LBM: +2.0±1.3% (slow) vs +0.8±1.1% (fast).
  - Upper-body strength: +12±2% vs +6±2%.
  - No between-group differences at 12 months.
  — [PubMed 21896944](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21896944/)
- [META] Murphy C, Koehler K 2022, *Scand J Med Sci Sports* 32(1):125–137, doi:10.1111/sms.14075, PMID 34623696. Studies: RT in an energy deficit for ≥3 weeks.
  - Lean mass vs non-deficit controls: ES −0.57 (p=0.02).
  - Strength: ES −0.31 (p=0.28, not significant).
  - Single-arm analysis: lean mass −0.11 in the deficit groups vs +0.20 in controls. Strength gains were similar (0.84 vs 0.81).
  - Meta-regression: a deficit of about 500 kcal/day prevented lean-mass gains.
  — [PubMed 34623696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)
- [NARR] Roberts et al. 2020, *J Hum Kinet* 71:79–108. Physique athletes. Fat 10–25% of kcal during contest prep, with remaining calories mostly from carbohydrate. The automated abstract extraction reported a target of ≤0.5% BW loss/week in the competition period. Verify this exact wording in the source. — [PubMed 32148575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148575/)
- [RCT] Peos JJ et al. 2021, ICECAP, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 53(8):1685–1698, doi:10.1249/MSS.0000000000002636, PMID 33587549. Design: n=61 resistance-trained adults (32 women), BW 77.2 kg, body fat 25.5%.
  - Intermittent arm: 4 × 3-week moderate energy-restriction blocks plus 3 × 1-week energy-balance blocks (15 weeks total).
  - Continuous arm: 12 weeks of continuous restriction.
  - Final fat mass: 15.3 vs 18.0 kg (p=0.321). FFM: 56.7 vs 56.7 kg.
  - The intermittent group had lower hunger (p=0.002) and higher satisfaction.
  - No differences in strength, REE, leptin, testosterone, IGF-1, or T3.
  — [PubMed 33587549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/)
- [Secondary analysis] Peos et al. 2021, *PLoS One* 16(2):e0247292, PMID 33630880. Design: n=26 trained athletes; a 1-week diet break at maintenance, raised mainly through carbohydrate.
  - BW +0.6 kg and FFM +0.7 kg, likely water and glycogen.
  - REE rose from 7,000 to 7,200 kJ/day (about 1,673 to 1,721 kcal).
  - Hunger fell. Strength did not change.
  — [PubMed 33630880](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33630880/)
- [RCT] Byrne NM et al. 2018, MATADOR, *Int J Obes* 42(2):129–138, doi:10.1038/ijo.2017.206, PMID 28925405. Design: men with obesity; 2-week on / 2-week off restriction vs continuous, both at 67% of maintenance during restriction.
  - Weight loss: 14.1 vs 9.1 kg. Fat loss: 12.3 vs 8.0 kg, favouring intermittent.
  - Body-composition-adjusted REE fell less with intermittent restriction (−360 vs −749 kJ/day).
  - Not a lifter population.
  — [PubMed 28925405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/)
- [NARR] Slater 2019: muscle protein synthesis was about 30% lower after 5 days at 30 kcal/kg FFM/day of restriction. — [Slater 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)
- [NARR] Slater & Phillips 2011, "Nutrition guidelines for strength sports," *J Sports Sci* 29(Suppl 1):S67–77, doi:10.1080/02640414.2011.574722, PMID 21660839. Strength athletes' per-kg intakes are "unremarkable." Recommendations should be individualized. Slater 2019 cites a carbohydrate range of 4–7 g/kg/day for strength athletes. — [PubMed 21660839](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660839/)

### Inferences
- App logic for a cut:
  - Default rate is 0.5–0.7% BW/week; the allowed range is 0.25–1.0%.
  - Hard warning above 1.0%/week sustained for 2 or more weeks.
  - Soft warning above 0.7%/week when estimated body fat is below about 12% (men) or about 20% (women). These leanness cut-offs are my inference from Helms 2014's "scale with leanness" principle, not published thresholds.
- Deficit: `kcal_deficit ≈ rate × BW × ρ_loss / 7`, with ρ_loss about 7,700 kcal/kg by default (lower in lean people; see Q4). Example: 80 kg at 0.5%/week = 0.4 kg/week ≈ 440 kcal/day. This sits right at the Murphy & Koehler about-500 kcal threshold. The app should tell users that hypertrophy is unlikely during the cut and the aim is to retain muscle.
- Macro order:
  1. Protein (Q1).
  2. Fat floor: max(0.5 g/kg, about 15–20% of kcal), default 20–30%.
  3. Carbohydrate is the remainder. Flag when it falls below about 3 g/kg in high-volume phases (low-certainty evidence; see Q9).
- Diet-break feature: offer 1-week maintenance blocks every 3–8 weeks as an adherence or hunger tool, not as a fat-loss accelerator (ICECAP). After a break, expect a bodyweight jump of about 0.5–1 kg from glycogen and water. The trend algorithm should not read this as fat regain.

### Gaps
- The deficit size used in ICECAP was not captured from the abstract.
- No trial directly compares loss rates in competitive powerlifters or women bodybuilders.
- Refeed (1–2 day) evidence in lifters was not retrieved this session.
- Escalante 2021 (peak week) was not retrieved.

## Q4. Energy-expenditure equations, activity multipliers, accuracy in lifters, and adaptive TDEE from intake plus weight trend

### Takeaway
For muscular athletes, prefer FFM-based or athlete-derived equations: Tinsley 2019, ten Haaf & Weijs 2014, and Cunningham. General-population equations (Mifflin, Harris-Benedict) usually underestimate RMR in physique athletes. Any equation is only a starting point. The app should back-calculate TDEE from logged intake and the smoothed weight trend: TDEE ≈ mean intake − ρ × Δtrend/Δt.

### Cited Findings
**Equations (kcal/day; W = kg, H = cm unless noted; A = years)**
- [Original] Mifflin MD et al. 1990, *Am J Clin Nutr* 51(2):241–247, doi:10.1093/ajcn/51.2.241, PMID 2305711. n=498 healthy adults aged 19–78.
  - Men: REE = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5. Women: REE = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161.
  - Combined: 9.99W + 6.25H − 4.92A + 166·sex − 161 (male = 1). R²=0.71.
  - FFM version: REE = 19.7·FFM + 413 (R²=0.64).
  - The 1919 Harris-Benedict equations overestimated measured REE by 5% in this sample.
  — [PubMed 2305711](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2305711/)
- [Athlete-derived] Tinsley GM, Graybeal AJ, Moore ML 2019, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 44(4):397–406, doi:10.1139/apnm-2018-0412, PMID 30240568. Population: 27 physique athletes (17 men at about 12.5% body fat; 10 women at about 19.2% body fat); indirect calorimetry.
  - New equations: RMR = 24.8 × BW(kg) + 10, and RMR = 25.9 × FFM(kg) + 284.
  - Most existing equations underestimated RMR in this group. The ten Haaf & Weijs and Cunningham (1980) FFM equations "may produce acceptable" estimates.
  — [PubMed 30240568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30240568/)
- [Athlete-derived] ten Haaf T, Weijs PJM 2014, *PLoS One* 9(10):e108460, doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0108460. Population: n=90 recreational athletes aged 18–35 (53 men, 37 women), training about 9 h/week. FFM measured by BodPod.
  - Weight-based equation (kcal/day): REE = 11.936·W(kg) + 587.728·H(**m**) − 8.129·A + 191.027·sex + 29.279, with sex = 1 for male and 0 for female. The original is in kJ: 49.940·W + 2459.053·H …; the kcal coefficients equal kJ ÷ 4.184.
  - FFM equation: REE = 22.771·FFM + 484.264 kcal/day.
  - Share of predictions within ±10% of measured REE: Cunningham 84.9% (men) / 78.4% (women); new weight-based 83.0% / 75.7%; new FFM-based 83.0% / 72.3%. Harris-Benedict, WHO, Schofield, Mifflin, and Owen were each below 50%.
  - RMSE for the FFM equation: 0.62 MJ (men) and 0.57 MJ (women); other general equations were above 0.95 MJ.
  — [PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108460); kcal coefficients from [Martinho et al. 2023, Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10286600/)
- [Original] Cunningham JJ 1991, *Am J Clin Nutr* 54(6):963–969, doi:10.1093/ajcn/54.6.963, PMID 1957828: REE = 370 + 21.6·FFM(kg). The abstract prints "370 ± 21.6" as a typo. FFM explains 65–90% of REE variance. This is the equation commonly sold as "Katch-McArdle." — [PubMed 1957828](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1957828/)
- [Original] Roza AM, Shizgal HM 1984, *Am J Clin Nutr* 40(1):168–182, doi:10.1093/ajcn/40.1.168, PMID 6741850. Re-derived Harris-Benedict equations. Precision is ±14% in normal subjects. — [PubMed 6741850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6741850/)
- [SR] Martinho DV et al. 2023, "Predicting resting energy expenditure among athletes: a systematic review," *Biol Sport* 40(3):787–804, doi:10.5114/biolsport.2023.119986. The De Lorenzo and ten Haaf & Weijs equations "seem to be the most appropriate" for adult athletes. Harris-Benedict and Cunningham agreement was often below 60% in athletes. General-population equations were "not comparable" to measured REE. — [PMC10286600](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10286600/)

**Energy density of weight change and back-calculation**
- [MODEL] Hall KD 2008, "What is the required energy deficit per unit weight loss?", *Int J Obes* 32(3):573–576, doi:10.1038/sj.ijo.0803720, PMID 17848938.
  - The 3,500 kcal/lb rule equals 32.2 MJ/kg (about 7,700 kcal/kg). It only roughly fits people with initial fat above about 30 kg.
  - Energy densities: fat ρF = 39.5 MJ/kg (about 9,440 kcal/kg); lean ρL = 7.6 MJ/kg (about 1,816 kcal/kg).
  - ΔE/ΔBW = ρF + (ρL − ρF)·(ΔL/ΔBW), where the lean fraction comes from a Forbes-type relation to initial fat mass.
  - Example: a 15 kg loss from 20 kg initial fat gives about 24.7 MJ/kg (about 5,900 kcal/kg). Leaner people need a lower deficit per kg lost.
  — [PMC2376744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2376744/)
- [Validation] Sanghvi A, Redman LM, Martin CK, Ravussin E, Hall KD 2015, *Am J Clin Nutr* 102(2):353–358, doi:10.3945/ajcn.115.111070, PMID 26040640. Population: n=140, CALERIE trial. A model using demographics and repeated body weights estimated the change in energy intake to within 40 kcal/day of doubly labelled water plus DXA over 2 years (mean). Individual RMSD was 215 kcal/day. This supports the principle of inferring energy balance from weight trajectories. — [PubMed 26040640](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26040640/)
- [NARR] Slater 2019, energy costs of gaining: about 5,000–5,200 kJ stored per kg of muscle; about 6,050–7,440 kJ/kg including synthesis costs. An overfeeding twin study gained about 2 kg fat per 1 kg lean. — [Slater 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)

### Inferences
- **Equation selection (app):**
  - If FFM is known (from user-entered body fat): RMR = mean(Tinsley FFM: 25.9·FFM + 284; ten Haaf FFM: 22.771·FFM + 484.264). Or pick one consistently.
  - Otherwise: ten Haaf weight-based (height in metres), or Tinsley BW: 24.8·BW + 10 for very muscular users.
  - Mifflin is a fallback for non-athletic users.
  - Show a ±10–15% uncertainty band.
- **TDEE seed = RMR × PAL.** Commonly used multipliers are sedentary 1.2, light 1.375, moderate 1.55, very active 1.725, and extra 1.9 (Harris-Benedict-era "Katch" factors). FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 PAL bands are about 1.40–1.69 (sedentary/light), 1.70–1.99 (active), and 2.00–2.40 (vigorous). UNVERIFIED this session; treat as seed only.
- **Adaptive TDEE (pseudo-code):**
  ```
  window = last 14–28 days with ≥ 70% intake-logging compliance
  intake_mean = mean(logged kcal over window)              // impute or exclude unlogged days
  dW = trend[end] − trend[start]                           // kg, from smoothed trend (Q5)
  rho = 7700 kcal/kg default; if cutting & lean: 5500–7000; if gaining with RT: ~5000–7000 (assumption)
  TDEE_est = intake_mean − rho × dW / days
  TDEE = blend(prior TDEE, TDEE_est) via Bayesian/Kalman-style update or EMA(α≈0.1–0.2 per week)
  ```
  The ρ values for lean or gaining states are inferences from Hall 2008 and Slater 2019, not validated constants. Errors in ρ cause small TDEE errors at typical rates. Example: 0.3 kg/week × 1,000 kcal/kg error ÷ 7 ≈ 43 kcal/day.
- Exclude or flag windows that contain glycogen and water perturbations:
  - starting creatine (+1–2 kg water, per IOC)
  - diet-break or refeed weeks (+0.5–0.7 kg, per Peos 2021)
  - the first 1–2 weeks of a large carbohydrate change
  - menstrual-cycle water shifts
  - powerlifting water cuts

### Gaps
- Coefficients UNVERIFIED this session:
  - Cunningham 1980: commonly 500 + 22·FFM.
  - Revised Harris-Benedict (Roza & Shizgal): commonly men 88.362 + 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A; women 447.593 + 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A.
  - De Lorenzo (male athletes): commonly −857 + 9.0W + 11.7H(cm). The Martinho table entry was truncated.
- Tinsley 2019 accuracy statistics (SEE, limits of agreement) were not captured.
- I found no peer-reviewed validation of a consumer "adaptive TDEE" algorithm in lifters. Commercial implementations such as MacroFactor are [PRACT] and were not reviewed.

## Q5. Bodyweight trend smoothing and day-to-day variability

### Takeaway
Daily bodyweight swings are mostly water and gut content. They typically range from about 0.5% to about 1.5% of BW per day, with weekly rhythms of about 0.35%. Smooth with an exponential moving average (EMA). The classic practitioner choice is α = 0.1 (a smoothing constant of 0.9, roughly a 20-day moving-average lag). Peer-reviewed validation of specific smoothing parameters is essentially absent, so label this as practical guidance.

### Cited Findings
- [OBS, classic] Khosla T, Billewicz WZ 1964, "Measurement of change in body weight," *Br J Nutr*. 19 subjects tracked for 30–40 days. Day-to-day fluctuation scaled with body size; the maximum one-day change "rarely exceeded" about 1.5% of BW (the OCR is imperfect). Earlier work (Durnin 1961, 44 men under controlled conditions) recorded day-to-day changes up to about 1 kg. A single-subject series had an SD of about 0.5% of BW. — [Cambridge PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4E78A0DB251133BEF39D73EB50C2F8A2/S0007114564000228a.pdf/div-class-title-measurement-of-change-in-body-weight-div.pdf)
- [OBS] Orsama AL et al. 2014, "Weight rhythms: weight increases during weekends and decreases during weekdays," *Obes Facts*, PMID 24504358. n=80 adults, 4,657 measurements. Weight is higher on Sunday and Monday and falls through the week. Within-week fluctuation is about 0.35%. — [PubMed 24504358](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24504358/)
- [PRACT] Walker J, *The Hacker's Diet*, chapter "Signal and Noise." Recommends an exponentially smoothed moving average with a smoothing constant of 0.9, "roughly equivalent to a 20 day simple moving average" in lag. Daily swings mostly reflect water in the "rubber bag." The trend lags the true value slightly. — [fourmilab.ch](https://www.fourmilab.ch/hackdiet/e4/signalnoise.html)

### Inferences
- EMA update for irregular sampling (my formulation; equivalent to Hacker's Diet when Δt = 1 day):
  ```
  α_day = 0.1                       // 0.1–0.15 for loss phases; 0.05–0.1 for slow bulks
  α_eff = 1 − (1 − α_day)^Δdays     // handles missed days
  trend = trend_prev + α_eff × (w_today − trend_prev)
  init: trend_0 = mean(first 3–7 weigh-ins)
  ```
- Outlier handling: winsorize single readings deviating more than about 2–2.5% from the current trend before updating. Example sources: post-cut rehydration, travel, sodium-heavy meals, menstrual phase. The threshold is an inference from the about-1.5% maximum daily change.
- Rate estimation: fit a linear regression to the trend (or to raw weights) over the last 14–28 days to get kg/week and %BW/week. This drives Q2/Q3 targets and Q4 TDEE.
- Standardize weigh-ins: morning, after voiding, before food or fluid, same scale. This is practical guidance; the studies above weighed under controlled conditions.
- Display: show the trend and the weekly rate as primary, with raw dots de-emphasized. Avoid daily-weight alerts.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study validating the EMA α or window choices for body-weight monitoring in athletes. Kalman-filter approaches were not reviewed.
- No sex-specific data were found on menstrual-cycle water fluctuation magnitude.

## Q6. Body-composition estimation without lab equipment (Navy, Jackson-Pollock, Siri/Brozek), FFMI and normalized FFMI, BMI limits

### Takeaway
Field methods carry about 3–4 percentage points of standard error, with systematic bias. The Navy circumference method tends to overestimate body fat in lean people, underestimate it in fatter people, and track change poorly. Treat estimated body fat as a trend indicator, not an absolute value. FFMI = FFM/height²; the normalized version adds 6.3 × (1.80 − height). The Kouri abstract says 6.3, while most calculators use 6.1. Non-users clustered at or below about 25.

### Cited Findings
- **US Navy circumference (Hodgdon & Beckett 1984a/b, Naval Health Research Center reports)**, as given in Potter 2022 (inches):
  - Men: %BF = 86.010·log10(abdomen − neck) − 70.041·log10(height) + 36.76. Abdomen is measured at the umbilicus.
  - Women: %BF = 163.205·log10(waist + hip − neck) − 97.684·log10(height) − 78.387. Potter's text prints "abdomen − neck" for women but lists the waist (narrowest), hip (greatest buttock protrusion), and neck as the measured sites, so the printed form looks like a transcription error. The commonly implemented female equation includes the hip.
  - Hodgdon equations have a reported SEE of about 3–4% body fat.
  — [Potter et al. 2022, Front Physiol 13:868627, doi:10.3389/fphys.2022.868627](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.868627/full)
- [Validation] Potter AW et al. 2022, *Front Physiol*. Population: US Marines, n=430 men and 179 women, aged 18–57; reference was DXA (GE iDXA).
  - Bias (method minus DXA): men −2.6 ± 3.7 (≤30 y) and −2.5 ± 3.7 (>30 y); women +2.3 ± 4.3 and +1.3 ± 4.8.
  - Concordance correlation: 0.51–0.57 in men, 0.72–0.74 in women.
  - The method overestimated body fat in leaner people and underestimated it in those with more fat.
  - BIA was more accurate.
  - Conclusion: the method suits obesity screening but does not provide research-grade data.
  - The search summary also mentions an Army trainee study (n=1,407) in which circumference methods failed to track 8-week change. I did not open that source.
  — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.868627/full)
- **Jackson-Pollock skinfolds.** The male 3-site equation uses intercept 1.10938, then Siri %BF = 495/BD − 450. In the original validation, the men's equation had R=0.905 and SEE of about 3.4% body fat; the women's had R=0.842 and SEE of about 3.9%. A DXA cross-validation in a diverse sample (TIGER study; Jackson AS et al. 2009, *Br J Nutr*) found the equations underestimated DXA body fat in Hispanic men and women and overestimated it in African-American men. Equations were derived mainly in White samples. — [PubMed 18702849](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18702849/) (search-result summary)
- [NARR] Kasper AM et al. 2021, "Come back skinfolds, all is forgiven," *Nutrients* 13(4):1075, PMID 33806245. No body-composition method is a gold standard in free-living people. The authors argue for raw skinfold sums in applied sport practice. — [PMC8065383](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8065383/)
- [POS/NARR] Mathisen TF et al. 2023, *Br J Sports Med* 57(17):1148–1158, PMID 37752006. Review of 29 studies plus a practitioner survey (n=125). No body-composition cut-off conferring a performance advantage was identified. Practitioners shifted away from emphasizing %BF (68% to 46%) and from frequent assessment (18% to 5% assessing every 4 weeks or more often). — [PubMed 37752006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752006/)
- **FFMI.** Kouri EM, Pope HG Jr, Katz DL, Oliva P 1995, *Clin J Sport Med* 5(4):223–228, doi:10.1097/00042752-199510000-00003, PMID 7496846.
  - Population: n=157 male athletes (83 anabolic-androgenic steroid users, 74 non-users), plus 20 Mr. America winners from 1939–1959.
  - FFMI = FFM(kg) × height(m)⁻². The abstract text reads "normalized" FFMI = FFMI + **6.3** × (1.80 − height).
  - Non-users' normalized FFMI "extended up to a well-defined limit of 25.0." The pre-steroid-era Mr. America mean was 25.4. Many users exceeded 25, and some exceeded 30.
  — [PubMed 7496846](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7496846/)

### Inferences
- Formulas for the app:
  - Metric Navy (UNVERIFIED this session; widely used conversion; cm, log10):
    - Men: %BF = 495 / (1.0324 − 0.19077·log10(waist − neck) + 0.15456·log10(height)) − 450.
    - Women: %BF = 495 / (1.29579 − 0.35004·log10(waist + hip − neck) + 0.22100·log10(height)) − 450.
    - Alternatively, convert cm to inches and use the verified inch equations above.
  - Jackson-Pollock (UNVERIFIED coefficients this session except the male 3-site intercept; S = sum of skinfolds in mm; A = years). Primary sources: Jackson & Pollock 1978, *Br J Nutr* 40:497–504; Jackson, Pollock & Ward 1980, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 12:175–181.
    - JP3 men (chest, abdomen, thigh): BD = 1.10938 − 0.0008267S + 0.0000016S² − 0.0002574A.
    - JP3 women (triceps, suprailiac, thigh): BD = 1.0994921 − 0.0009929S + 0.0000023S² − 0.0001392A.
    - JP7 men: BD = 1.112 − 0.00043499S + 0.00000055S² − 0.00028826A.
    - JP7 women: BD = 1.097 − 0.00046971S + 0.00000056S² − 0.00012828A.
    - Siri: %BF = 495/BD − 450. Brozek: %BF = 457/BD − 414.2 (Brozek UNVERIFIED).
  - FFM = BW × (1 − %BF/100). FFMI = FFM / H². Normalized FFMI = FFMI + k × (1.80 − H). k = 6.3 per the Kouri abstract; 6.1 is the common implementation. The difference is negligible (0.02 units at H = 1.70 m). Choose one and document it.
- Display guidance:
  - Present %BF with an uncertainty band of about ±3–4 points and a "trend only" label.
  - Encourage raw skinfold sums or waist circumference trends (Kasper 2021).
  - Do not compute FFMI for users under 18.
  - Label the FFMI of about 25 as an observation in drug-free men from a single 1995 sample, not a hard limit or doping test.
- BMI misclassifies lifters. Worked example: a 1.80 m man at FFMI 25 and 10% body fat weighs 90 kg, giving BMI 27.8, which counts as "overweight." The app should not use BMI for lifters' health messaging.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the primary texts of the Jackson-Pollock or Brozek coefficients, or the metric Navy conversion.
- No validation of the Navy or JP equations against DXA or 4-compartment models was found specifically in competitive bodybuilders or powerlifters.
- No female FFMI "natural limit" reference data were found.
- I could not access the Kouri full text to resolve 6.1 vs 6.3, or to confirm how FFM was measured (likely skinfolds).

## Q7. Powerlifting weight classes: acute weight loss prevalence, safe magnitudes by weigh-in timing, rehydration and refuelling

### Takeaway
Most powerlifters cut acutely: 86–97% do so, typically by about 3–4% of BW, mainly through fluid restriction and water loading. ACSM 2021 says losses of about 2–3% BW via fluid or sweat have "few disadvantages" if aggressive recovery follows. Losses above 3% risk substantial health and performance harm. IPF weigh-ins are at least 2 h before lifting, so recovery time is short. The best RCT (Matras 2025) found no strength loss after a 4-day, about 4.8% cut with 2 h recovery. Rehydrate with about 150% of the fluid deficit, plus sodium and carbohydrate.

### Cited Findings
- [OBS] Nolan D, Lynch AE, Egan B 2022, *J Strength Cond Res* 36(2):405–410, doi:10.1519/JSC.0000000000003488, PMID 31904717. n=321 competitive powerlifters (194 men, 127 women).
  - Prevalence of rapid weight loss (RWL): 85.8%. Mean RWL: 3.0 ± 1.9% BW. Analysis subgroup: ≤2-h weigh-in, drug-tested, raw (n=200).
  - "Always used" methods: fluid restriction 54.0%, water loading 49.0%.
  - Coaches (37.5%) and online resources (35.0%) were "very influential." Doctors and dietitians were rated "not influential."
  — [PubMed 31904717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31904717/)
- [OBS] Campbell P, Martin D, Bargh MJ, Gee TI 2025, *Nutr Health* 31(2):565–574 (epub 2023), doi:10.1177/02601060231201892. n=69 IPF lifters from Great Britain (<2-h weigh-in).
  - 97% cut. Typical loss: 4.2%. Regional vs international: 5.5% vs 3.3%.
  - Highest-ever loss: women 6.7% vs men 5.3%.
  - Methods: fluid restriction 86.5%, water loading 67.2%, extra exercise 49.2%.
  - About 70% of perceptions were negative (fatigue, anxiety, low mood).
  - The authors caution against losses above 5%.
  — [PubMed 37697737](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37697737/)
- [RCT] Matras A, Czapla M, Struzik A 2025, *JISSN* 22(1):2550309, doi:10.1080/15502783.2025.2550309, PMID 40864504. Design: trained male powerlifters, n=26 (13 cut, 13 controls).
  - Protocol: 4 days of a 10% caloric deficit, carbohydrate <50 g/day, low sodium, and fluid manipulation, targeting 5% loss; then a 2-h recovery.
  - Loss: −4.81% BW (fat-free mass −2.36%, body water −2.41%).
  - No significant change in squat, bench, deadlift, or total. IPF GL points rose because of the lower bodyweight.
  — [PubMed 40864504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40864504/)
- [OBS] Kwan K, King A, Helms ER 2026, *Res Q Exerc Sport* (online ahead of print), doi:10.1080/02701367.2026.2635723, PMID 41894735. n=53 elite lifters at the 2019 and 2022 IPF Worlds.
  - Mean loss from 7 days out to day 0: −2.5% (1.83 kg).
  - Greater relative loss was associated with worse stress and recovery scores. Women showed worse negative-emotional-state responses at higher losses.
  — [PubMed 41894735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41894735/)
- [POS] Burke LM, Slater GJ, Matthews JJ, Langan-Evans C, Horswill CA 2021, "ACSM Expert Consensus Statement on Weight Loss in Weight-Category Sports," *Curr Sports Med Rep* 20(4):199–217, doi:10.1249/JSR.0000000000000831, PMID 33790193.
  - Fluid restriction or sweat loss to "2% to 3% BM reduction" is "likely to have few disadvantages, especially if aggressive recovery strategies are implemented." A "singular focus" on these practices for larger losses (>3% BM) "may have substantial negative implications to health and performance."
  - IPF rules: weigh-in no less than 2 h before the first event; one official attempt.
  - Rehydration (Table 3): about 150% of the fluid deficit; replace sodium (oral rehydration solution or about 50–60 mmol/L sodium, or salty foods); a 10 mL/kg bolus immediately after weigh-in, then smaller regular volumes. Adding protein aids fluid retention.
  - Refuelling: about 1 g/kg/h carbohydrate early in recovery, moderate-to-high-GI, compact low-fat and low-fibre choices; about 20–25 g protein. Recovery may be incomplete if the window is only 1–2 h after severe carbohydrate restriction or exercise.
  - A lightweight-rowing example 2-h recovery protocol: 2.3 g/kg carbohydrate, 25–28 mL/kg fluid, and 34 mg/kg sodium.
  - Discouraged or banned practices: saunas, plastic suits, laxatives, emetics, diuretics, and IV rehydration (banned by sport governing bodies).
  - The NCAA wrestling model capped weight loss at ≤1.5% BM/week.
  — [LJMU accepted manuscript PDF](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/17789/1/ACSM%20Consensus%20Statement%20Weight%20Making.pdf); [PubMed 33790193](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33790193/)
- [NARR] Reale R, Slater G, Burke LM 2017, *Int J Sports Physiol Perform* 12(2):142–151, doi:10.1123/ijspp.2016-0211, PMID 27347784. Offers a "decision tree" for acute body-mass loss that minimizes performance decrements. The thresholds are not in the abstract. — [PubMed 27347784](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27347784/)

### Inferences
- App "meet cut planner" logic:
  - Inputs: meet date, weigh-in type (2 h, as in IPF, or 24 h, as in some federations), class limit, and current trend weight.
  - Required loss % = (trend − limit)/trend.
  - Chronic portion: anything above about 3% (2-h weigh-in) or about 5% (24-h weigh-in) should come from a chronic diet over weeks at ≤0.5–1%/week (Q3). The 24-h figure is an inference from the longer recovery window plus Campbell's >5% caution; it is not an ACSM number.
  - Acute portion: ≤2–3% for 2-h weigh-ins. Warn at >3%. Strong warning or block at >5%, or for any sauna, diuretic, or laxative plan.
- Post-weigh-in checklist:
  - Fluid ≈ 1.5 × kg lost (L), starting with a 10 mL/kg bolus.
  - Sodium-containing oral rehydration solution.
  - Carbohydrate about 1 g/kg/h; a 2-h template is about 2–2.5 g/kg total.
  - Protein 20–25 g.
  - Low fibre and low fat.
- Weight-class suggestion: recommend the class where the user's off-season trend sits within about 2–3% above the limit, or below it.

### Gaps
- Exact Reale 2017 decision-tree thresholds (for example, ≤2% via dehydration for short recovery, more for 24 h) were not verified. I found no validated powerlifting-specific maximum for 24-h weigh-ins.
- Matras 2025 included only men. Female cut-performance data are lacking.

## Q8. Supplements with strong evidence (creatine, caffeine), and brief notes on beta-alanine, citrulline, and vitamin D (IOC evidence grades)

### Takeaway
Only creatine monohydrate and caffeine have strong evidence relevant to lifting. The IOC lists them among the few supplements with good evidence; the others are buffering agents and nitrate, which are less relevant to strength. Beta-alanine targets 30 s to 10 min efforts and matters little for 1RM work. Citrulline has a small effect (g ≈ 0.2). Vitamin D is useful only to correct a deficiency.

### Cited Findings
- [POS] Kreider RB et al. 2017, ISSN position stand on creatine, *JISSN* 14:18, doi:10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z, PMID 28615996. Creatine up to 30 g/day for 5 years is described as safe and well tolerated. — [PubMed 28615996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/)
- [POS] IOC (Maughan RJ et al. 2018), *Br J Sports Med* 52(7):439–455, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027, PMID 29540367. Also published in *IJSNEM* 28(2):104–125.
  - Only a few supplements "(including caffeine, creatine, specific buffering agents and nitrate) have good evidence of benefits."
  - Creatine: loading about 20 g/day in 4 doses for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g/day maintenance. Co-ingesting about 50 g protein and carbohydrate may improve uptake. Expect a 1–2 kg body-mass gain, mainly water. Classed as "good to strong evidence."
  - Caffeine: 3–6 mg/kg about 60 min before exercise. Low doses below 3 mg/kg (about 200 mg) also work. Doses of 9 mg/kg or more add no benefit and increase side effects.
  - Beta-alanine: about 65 mg/kg/day in 0.8–1.6 g doses every 3–4 h for 10–12 weeks. Gains of about 0.2–3% in 30 s to 10 min tasks. Causes paraesthesia.
  - Vitamin D: for deficient athletes, 50,000 IU/week for 8–16 weeks or 10,000 IU/day for several weeks, with toxicity monitoring. Performance effects are equivocal.
  - Contamination: one study found about 15% of more than 600 products contained undeclared prohormones. Third-party testing reduces but does not remove the risk.
  — [PMC5867441](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/); [PubMed 29540367](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29540367/)
- [META] Delpino FM et al. 2022, *Nutrition* 103–104:111791, doi:10.1016/j.nut.2022.111791, PMID 35986981. 35 RCTs, n=1,192.
  - Creatine increased LBM by +0.68 kg (95% CI 0.26–1.11) overall and by +1.10 kg (0.56–1.65) with RT.
  - Men: +1.46 kg (0.47–2.46). Women: +0.29 kg (−0.43 to 1.01, not significant).
  — [PubMed 35986981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35986981/)
- [META] Lanhers C et al. 2017, *Sports Med* 47(1):163–173, doi:10.1007/s40279-016-0571-4, PMID 27328852. 53 RCTs (563 creatine, 575 placebo). Upper-limb strength ES 0.317 (95% CI 0.185–0.449). Bench press ES 0.265 (0.132–0.398). — [PubMed 27328852](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328852/)
- [POS] Guest NS et al. 2021, ISSN position stand on caffeine, *JISSN* 18:1, doi:10.1186/s12970-020-00383-4, PMID 33388079. 3–6 mg/kg consistently improves performance; the minimal effective dose may be about 2 mg/kg; 9 mg/kg brings more side effects without added benefit. The most common timing is 60 min before exercise. — [PubMed 33388079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/)
- [POS] Trexler ET et al. 2015, ISSN position stand on beta-alanine, *JISSN* 12:30, doi:10.1186/s12970-015-0090-y, PMID 26175657. 4–6 g/day for at least 2–4 weeks; divided doses of about 1.6 g or sustained-release formulas reduce paraesthesia. Largest benefit in 1–4 min tasks. Effects on strength need more research. — [PubMed 26175657](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26175657/)
- [META] Trexler ET et al. 2019, *Sports Med* 49(5):707–718, doi:10.1007/s40279-019-01091-z, PMID 30895562. 12 studies, n=198, citrulline at least 3 g taken at least 30 min before exercise. Strength and power: Hedges' g = 0.20 (95% CI 0.01–0.39). — [PubMed 30895562](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30895562/)

### Inferences
- Supplement cards for the app:
  - **Creatine monohydrate**, strong evidence: 3–5 g/day, or optional loading of about 0.3 g/kg/day (≈20 g/day at 70 kg) in 4 doses for 5–7 days. Expected LBM about +1 kg over a training block, with water weight of 1–2 kg early. Tag the start date so trend and TDEE logic can discount the water gain.
  - **Caffeine**, strong evidence: 3–6 mg/kg about 60 min before training. Cap suggestions below 9 mg/kg. Warn about sleep if taken late (general guidance).
  - **Beta-alanine**, moderate evidence and low relevance to 1RM work: 4–6 g/day.
  - **Citrulline**, small effect: 6–8 g citrulline malate before training.
  - **Vitamin D**: test-and-treat only.
- Add a contamination warning recommending third-party-tested products for drug-tested lifters.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the Kreider 2017 full-text loading protocol wording ("0.3 g/kg/day for 5–7 days") from the primary source; the IOC's 20 g/day is the verified equivalent.
- Wang 2024 and Burke 2023 creatine hypertrophy meta-analyses were not retrieved.
- I did not search for formal IOC "A/B/C" grades; the 2018 consensus uses evidence categories rather than letter grades.

## Q9. Nutrient timing, peri-workout nutrition, and carbohydrate intake for resistance-training performance

### Takeaway
Timing effects are small once total daily protein is matched (Schoenfeld 2013). Carbohydrate has little effect on typical strength performance. It may help higher-volume sessions or glycogen-depleted states (Henselmans 2022). A 2026 meta-analysis found no independent hypertrophy benefit from higher carbohydrate intake (low certainty).

### Cited Findings
- [SR] Henselmans M, Bjørnsen T, Hedderman R, Vårvik FT 2022, *Nutrients* 14(4):856, doi:10.3390/nu14040856, PMID 35215506. 49 studies.
  - Acute studies: 13 showed no benefit and 6 showed a benefit, with no dose-response. Higher carbohydrate helped in 3 glycogen-depletion studies.
  - Short-term (2–7 days): 0 of 7 studies found a benefit. Long-term: 15 of 17 found no effect.
  - "Performance during higher volumes may benefit from carbohydrates."
  — [PubMed 35215506](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35215506/)
- [META] Henselmans M, Vårvik FT, Izquierdo M 2026, *Sports Med* 56(3):691–702, doi:10.1007/s40279-025-02341-z, PMID 41712097. 11 RCTs comparing higher vs lower carbohydrate. Hypertrophy SMD 0.15 (p=0.23), and 0.15 in isocaloric trials (p=0.60). GRADE certainty: low. — [PubMed 41712097](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41712097/)
- [META] Schoenfeld, Aragon, Krieger 2013: protein timing had no significant effect once total protein was controlled. — [PubMed 24299050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/)
- [POS] Jäger 2017: 20–40 g protein every 3–4 h, plus 30–40 g casein pre-sleep. — [PubMed 28642676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- [NARR] Roberts 2020: one protein feeding near training and one before sleep. — [PubMed 32148575](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32148575/)
- [NARR] Slater 2019: carbohydrate 4–7 g/kg/day for strength athletes. RT reduces muscle glycogen by 30–40%. — [Slater 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)

### Inferences
- The app should de-emphasize strict timing windows. Use a "protein in the meal before or after training" nudge and an optional pre-sleep protein suggestion.
- Show a carbohydrate guide of about 3–7 g/kg (default 3–5 g/kg in a cut, 4–7 g/kg in a bulk or high-volume block), noted as low certainty. Never place carbohydrate targets ahead of protein or the energy target.

### Gaps
- The Kerksick 2017 ISSN nutrient-timing position stand was not retrieved.
- No powerlifting-specific peri-competition carbohydrate trials were found beyond the weight-cut literature.

## Q10. Sex-specific considerations (menstrual cycle, and female data in the trials above)

### Takeaway
Current evidence does not support adjusting strength training or nutrition targets by menstrual-cycle phase. The effects found are trivial or absent and the underlying evidence is low quality, so individual tracking is the sensible approach. Women were under-represented in most of the trials above, and some effects (creatine on LBM) were not significant in women.

### Cited Findings
- [META] McNulty KL et al. 2020, *Sports Med* 50(10):1813–1827, doi:10.1007/s40279-020-01319-3, PMID 32661839. 78 studies of eumenorrheic women. Performance in the early follicular phase vs all other phases: ES −0.06 (95% CrI −0.16 to 0.04), a trivial reduction. Evidence quality was rated "low." The authors recommend individualized approaches. — [PubMed 32661839](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32661839/)
- [Umbrella review] Colenso-Semple LM, D'Souza AC, Elliott-Sale KJ, Phillips SM 2023, *Front Sports Act Living* 5:1054542, doi:10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542, PMID 37033884. No influence of menstrual-cycle phase on acute strength or RT adaptations was found. It is premature to say short-term hormone changes matter. — [PubMed 37033884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37033884/)
- [RCT crossover] Colenso-Semple LM et al. 2025, *J Physiol* 603(5):1109–1121, doi:10.1113/JP287342, PMID 39630025. n=12 women. Myofibrillar protein synthesis did not differ between the late follicular and mid-luteal phases. — [PubMed 39630025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39630025/)
- Creatine and LBM in women: +0.29 kg, not significant (Delpino 2022). — [PubMed 35986981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35986981/)
- Weight cuts: women report larger maximum losses (6.7% vs 5.3%; Campbell 2025) and worse stress responses at higher losses (Kwan 2026). — [PubMed 37697737](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37697737/); [PubMed 41894735](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41894735/)
- ICECAP included 32 of 61 women, and diet-break findings did not differ by sex in the abstract. — [PubMed 33587549](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/)

### Inferences
- Offer optional cycle logging, used only to annotate weight-trend noise and subjective readiness, not to change macro targets.
- Use female-specific Navy and JP equations and the -161 Mifflin constant.
- Apply RED-S screening prompts (Q11) to all users, with particular emphasis for women.

### Gaps
- Female-specific protein, surplus, and deficit-rate trials in trained lifters are scarce.
- No female FFMI norms were found.

## Q11. Safety thresholds the app should warn about (RED-S and energy availability, extreme deficits, rapid weight cuts, supplement dosing)

### Takeaway
Build guardrails around several thresholds:
- energy availability below about 30 kcal/kg FFM/day
- sustained loss above 1% BW/week (with ≤1.5%/week as an absolute regulatory-style cap)
- acute weight cuts above 3% BW, and especially above 5%
- dehydration methods such as saunas, diuretics, laxatives, and vomiting
- caffeine at 9 mg/kg or more

### Cited Findings
- Energy availability formula: EA = (energy intake − exercise energy expenditure)/FFM. Below 30 kcal/kg FFM/day is generally classed as "low"; about 45 is considered "optimal" (balance in healthy women). The 30 threshold comes from studies of young, lean, sedentary women and is debated; a later paper argued that a single absolute threshold is not supported. — [Areta, Taylor, Koehler 2021 review (LJMU PDF)](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/14136/1/Low%20energy%20availability%20history,%20definition%20and%20evidence%20of%20its%20endocrine,%20metabolic%20and%20physiological%20effects%20in%20prospective%20studies%20in%20females%20and%20males.pdf)
- 2023 IOC REDs consensus: Mountjoy M et al., *Br J Sports Med* 57(17):1073–1097, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994, PMID 37752011. It includes the REDs CAT2 clinical assessment tool with traffic-light risk stratification (Stellingwerff 2023, PMID 37752002). — [PubMed 37752011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)
- Muscle protein synthesis fell about 30% after 5 days at 30 kcal/kg FFM/day (Slater 2019). Lean-mass gains are blocked at deficits of about 500 kcal/day or more (Murphy & Koehler 2022). — [Slater 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full); [PubMed 34623696](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/)
- ACSM 2021: acute fluid-based losses above 3% BM may carry substantial health and performance risks. Deaths have been reported with extreme practices. The NCAA cap is ≤1.5% BM/week. Saunas, plastic suits, laxatives, emetics, and diuretics are discouraged or banned. — [ACSM 2021 PDF](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/17789/1/ACSM%20Consensus%20Statement%20Weight%20Making.pdf)
- Caffeine at 9 mg/kg or more brings more side effects without added benefit (Guest 2021; IOC 2018). Creatine up to 30 g/day for 5 years is described as safe (Kreider 2017). Vitamin D megadoses need monitoring (IOC 2018). — [PubMed 33388079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33388079/); [PubMed 28615996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28615996/); [PMC5867441](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/)
- Fat intake: dropping from 33.3% to 13.9% of energy lowered resting testosterone; keep fat at or above about 15–20% of energy (Slater 2019). — [Slater 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2019.00131/full)

### Inferences
- Suggested guardrails (thresholds are evidence-informed design choices, not validated clinical cut-offs):
  1. Compute EA when FFM and training energy expenditure are available. Warn below 30 kcal/kg FFM/day; soft-warn between 30 and 45 during cuts.
  2. Warn when the weekly trend loss is above 1.0% BW for 2 or more weeks. Show a strong warning above 1.5%/week.
  3. Warn when a planned kcal target falls below estimated RMR. This is a practitioner convention, not sourced here.
  4. Flag fat intake below 0.5 g/kg or below 15% of kcal.
  5. Meet-cut planner: warn when the acute cut is above 3% of BW; strong warning or refusal above 5%; always discourage saunas, diuretics, laxatives, vomiting, and IV rehydration.
  6. Cap caffeine suggestions below 9 mg/kg.
  7. Prompt users to see a clinician for amenorrhea, recurrent injury or bone stress, or disordered-eating cues (RED-S screening).
  8. Show an "information, not medical advice" disclaimer.

### Gaps
- The 2023 IOC REDs consensus abstract gives no numeric EA threshold, and I did not read its full text this session.
- No validated minimum-calorie floor for lifters was found. The common 1,200/1,500 kcal floors are practitioner conventions and unsourced here.
